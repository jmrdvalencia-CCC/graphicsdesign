    How RGB and CMYK work

    Two completely different physical processes — one builds color with light, the other removes it with ink.

    🖥️ RGB — Additive Process

    What it is: A color system for computer screens, phones, and TVs.

    Base state: Black — the screen is off and no light shows.

    How it works: It adds red, green, and blue light together.

    Result: Adding all three full lights together makes white.

    🖨️ CMYK — Subtractive Process

    What it is: A color system for printed paper and magazines.

    Base state: White paper, which reflects all light back to your eyes.

    How it works: It uses cyan, magenta, yellow, and black ink.

    Result: Adding inks subtracts colors from the reflected light.

    How the subtractive method happens

    Step by step — what physically occurs when ink sits on paper.

    1. White light hits the paper — White light from the sun or a lamp shines down on printed paper.
    2. Inks act as filters — Tiny ink dots sit on the paper and act as color filters.
    3. Cyan absorbs red — Cyan ink blocks and takes in red light, letting blue and green light bounce back.
    4. Magenta absorbs green — Magenta ink blocks and takes in green light, letting blue and red light bounce back.
    5. Yellow absorbs blue — Yellow ink blocks and takes in blue light, letting red and green light bounce back.
    6. Color is subtracted — Mixing these inks blocks more light types, leaving fewer colors to bounce back to your eyes.
    7. Darkness appears — Mixing cyan, magenta, and yellow absorbs almost all light, making a dark brown or muddy color.
    8. Black ink helps — Printers add black ink to make true dark shades and save colored ink.
    Why K and not B? In CMYK, black is called K (Key) to avoid confusion with Blue in RGB. The "key" plate in traditional printing carried the sharpest detail — usually printed in black.
