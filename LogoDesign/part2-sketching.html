Part 2 · Section 2.1

Why Sketch Before Software?

Paper is the fastest prototyping tool a designer has. Sketching forces thinking in concepts, not clicks.

The Core Reason Sketching allows 20 ideas in the time it takes to build one on screen. Bad ideas are cheaper to discard on paper. It removes the temptation to polish too early — which locks designers into weak concepts.
Key Concept The goal of sketching is not to produce a finished drawing. It is to externalize as many ideas as possible as quickly as possible, so the best concept can be identified before any time is invested in execution.

Sketching achieves:

  • Forces thinking in concepts, not software features
  • Allows 20 ideas in the time it takes to build one on screen
  • Makes it cheap and fast to discard bad ideas
  • Produces a record of the design process — essential for client presentations
  • Removes the temptation to polish too early
  • Prevents locking into weak concepts before exploring alternatives
Part 2 · Section 2.2

The Thumbnail Sketching Process

A structured 5-step process — the professional standard for logo ideation.

  1. Brief Analysis — Read the design brief carefully. Highlight: industry and business type, target audience (age, gender, lifestyle, values), brand personality, competitors' logos, and any mandatory elements.
  2. Word Association — Write 20–30 words related to the brand: what it does, what it feels like, what it values. Circle the 5–8 most visually suggestive words — words that immediately suggest a shape, symbol, or letterform.
  3. Thumbnail Grid — Draw a grid of 20 small boxes (4 columns × 5 rows, ≈ 3 cm each). Fill every box — one idea per box. No erasing. No color. No judgment — quantity is the goal. Vary approaches: symbol, letterform, abstract shape, combination.
  4. Select & Refine — Review all 20 thumbnails. Circle the 3–5 strongest concepts. Redraw each at a larger size (≈ 8–10 cm) with more detail and precision.
  5. Concept Presentation — Present 2–3 refined sketches to the client or peer group before digitizing. This prevents wasted digitizing time on a concept the client will reject.

Tools for Sketching

The right tool for each stage of the sketching process — from loose ideation to refined line-art ready for scanning.

ToolBest For
HB or 2B pencilLoose thumbnails, quick ideation, easy to see light marks
Fine-liner pen (0.3–0.5 mm)Refined line-art before scanning; clean, consistent line weight
Marker or brush penBold, gestural letterforms; testing thick/thin stroke contrast
Grid or dot paperGeometric and symmetrical logos; maintaining proportions
Tracing paperIterating on top of a base sketch without redrawing from scratch
Pro Tip Start every session with a pencil — never a pen. Pencil marks are fast, light, and commitment-free. Switch to a fine-liner only when refining the 3–5 strongest thumbnails.

Line as a Design Element

Every logo begins as a line. The character of the line communicates personality before any color or typography is applied.

Line TypeCommunicates
Straight horizontalStability, calm, groundedness
Straight verticalStrength, authority, formality
DiagonalEnergy, movement, dynamism
CurvedFriendliness, flow, approachability
Jagged / angularTension, aggression, excitement

Why Line Matters First

Line is the most fundamental element of a sketch. Before a shape is closed, before color is chosen, the line already communicates. A logo built on horizontal lines feels stable and calm. The same mark redrawn with diagonals feels urgent and dynamic.

This is why the brief must inform the line choice — not personal preference or current trends.

Sketching Application In your thumbnail grid, consciously vary the dominant line direction across different boxes. Try the same concept with horizontal lines, then diagonal, then curved — and observe how the personality shifts.

Shape as a Design Element

Enclosed lines form shapes. Shape is the primary carrier of meaning in a logo symbol — it communicates before the viewer reads a single word.

ShapeCommunicates
Circle / ovalUnity, wholeness, community, protection, infinity
Square / rectangleStability, reliability, professionalism, structure
TriangleDirection, power, ambition, tension — point up = strength; point down = instability
Organic / irregularNature, creativity, handmade quality, uniqueness

Design Application

Before sketching, decide what the logo's primary shape language should be — from the brief, not from personal preference.

Financial institution → squares and rectangles (stability, reliability). Children's brand → circles and organic shapes (friendliness, warmth). Sports brand → triangles and diagonals (energy, direction).

Key Principle The strongest logos are recognizable as pure black silhouettes. If the shape does not communicate the brand without color, the form is not strong enough. Build shape first — add color second.
Part 2 · Section 2.5

Do's & Don'ts

Common mistakes that stall the sketching process — and the habits that keep it moving.

✓ Do

  • Sketch fast — quantity over quality in the thumbnail phase
  • Vary approaches: geometric, organic, typographic, abstract
  • Think in silhouette — check if the shape reads as a solid black form
  • Date and number every page of your sketchbook
  • Keep all sketches — rejected ideas sometimes become useful later

✗ Don't

  • Don't erase — cross out and move on; erasing wastes time
  • Don't add color at the thumbnail stage — color distracts from form
  • Don't open design software until you have a chosen, refined concept
  • Don't show the client only one concept — always present at least two or three
  • Don't skip the brief analysis — designing without understanding the audience produces generic work

Design Thinking

The Rule of 20

A foundational technique in creative problem-solving and brainstorming, popularised by design thinking and ideation frameworks. Here is why forcing yourself to reach 20 ideas works:

Step 1 — Ideas 1–5 · The Habitual Zone

Your brain is an energy-saving organ. When faced with a prompt, it immediately retrieves existing mental models, clichés, and things you have seen recently. These are predictable, low-friction answers that almost anyone in your field would come up with.

Step 2 — Ideas 6–12 · The Adaptation Zone

Variations and tweaks on the first few ideas. You are taking the obvious concepts and reframing, combining, or stretching them. Still derivative, but getting warmer.

Step 3 — Ideas 13–15 · The Wall

Around ideas 13–15 you hit a mental wall. You run out of immediate memories to modify and feel genuinely stuck. To move past this threshold, your brain has to stop searching memory and start forcing unexpected connections between disparate concepts.

Step 4 — Ideas 16–20 · The Lateral Thinking Zone

Once the safe, conventional options are off the table, the risk of proposing something “weird” drops to zero. You are forced to break rules, reverse premises, or look at extreme edge cases. The pressure to be “correct” gives way to novelty — and the rare gem hidden in this final batch is almost always more valuable than the safe choices in the first five.
