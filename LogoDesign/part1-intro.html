Part 1 · Section 1.1

What is Logo Design?

A logo is a graphic mark, emblem, or symbol used to identify a company, organization, product, or individual — the most concentrated expression of a brand's identity.

Logo vs. Brand

A logo is not the brand itself — it is the symbol of the brand. Its power grows from the story, quality, and reputation it represents over time. The Nike swoosh means nothing without Nike's history behind it.

Key Concept Every design principle — line, shape, color, typography, proportion, and space — converges in a logo. A well-designed logo is not decoration; it is a strategic communication tool.

Learning Objectives

  1. Define logo design and explain its role as a visual element of brand identity.
  2. Identify the five types of logos and describe the appropriate context for each.
  3. Apply a structured sketching process to generate original logo concepts.
  4. Digitize a hand-drawn sketch into clean vector artwork.
  5. Explain scalability and demonstrate why vector format is required.
  6. Select the correct file format for each logo use case.
Part 1 · Section 1.2

Five Types of Logos

Typography and symbol are the two primary components of logo design. These combine — or stand alone — to produce five distinct types.

TypeDescriptionExamples
WordmarkBrand name in a distinctive custom typeface. No symbol — the typography is the logo.Google, Coca-Cola, FedEx, Disney
LettermarkInitials or abbreviations styled as a graphic mark. Used when the full name is too long.IBM, NASA, HBO, LV
BrandmarkA purely graphic symbol with no text. Requires strong brand recognition to work alone.Apple, Twitter bird, Nike swoosh, Shell
Combination MarkWordmark or lettermark paired with a symbol. Most versatile — elements can be used together or separately.Adidas, Burger King, Lacoste, Mastercard
EmblemText enclosed within a symbol, badge, or seal. Feels official and traditional. Less flexible at small sizes.Starbucks, Harley-Davidson, BMW, NFL
Design Note For most new brands, a combination mark is the recommended starting point — it gives both a recognizable symbol and a readable name that can be separated as the brand grows.
Part 1 · Section 1.3

Qualities of an Effective Logo

First articulated by designer Paul Rand and widely adopted in the field. These are functional requirements, not aesthetic preferences.

QualityWhat It MeansWhy It Matters
Simple Uses the fewest elements necessary to communicate the idea Complex logos fail at small sizes and are hard to remember
Memorable Distinct enough to be recalled after a single viewing Recognition is the primary function of a logo
Timeless Avoids trends that will date the design within a few years A logo should work as well in 20 years as it does today
Versatile Works in color, black-and-white, reversed, and at any size Logos appear on everything from favicons to billboards
Appropriate Fits the brand's industry, audience, and personality A children's toy brand and a law firm require very different visual languages
Critical Thinking The Coca-Cola logo has remained essentially unchanged since 1887. What qualities make it timeless? What would happen to brand recognition if it were redesigned in a current trend style every five years?
