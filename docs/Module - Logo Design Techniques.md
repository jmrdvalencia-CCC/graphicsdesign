## PART 1: WHAT IS LOGO DESIGN? (20 minutes)

### 1.1 Definition

A **logo** is a graphic mark, emblem, or symbol used to identify a company, organization, product, or individual. It is the most concentrated expression of a brand's identity — a single visual element that must communicate personality, values, and recognition instantly and across every medium.

According to *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)*, graphic design is the practice of visual communication, and the logo is its most distilled form. Every design principle — line, shape, color, typography, proportion, and space — converges in a logo. A well-designed logo is not decoration; it is a strategic communication tool.

> **Key Concept:** A logo is not the brand itself — it is the *symbol* of the brand. Its power grows from the story, quality, and reputation it represents over time. The Nike swoosh means nothing without Nike's history behind it. The designer's job is to create a mark worthy of that story.

---

### 1.2 The Five Types of Logos

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* identify typography and symbol as the two primary components of logo design. These components combine — or stand alone — to produce five distinct logo types.

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Wordmark (Logotype)** | The brand name set in a distinctive, custom typeface. No symbol — the typography *is* the logo. | Google, Coca-Cola, FedEx, Disney |
| **Lettermark (Monogram)** | Initials or abbreviations of the brand name, styled as a graphic mark. Used when the full name is too long. | IBM, NASA, HBO, LV (Louis Vuitton) |
| **Brandmark (Pictorial Mark)** | A purely graphic symbol with no text. Requires strong brand recognition to work alone. | Apple, Twitter (bird), Nike swoosh, Shell |
| **Combination Mark** | A wordmark or lettermark paired with a symbol. The most versatile type — elements can be used together or separately. | Adidas, Burger King, Lacoste, Mastercard |
| **Emblem** | Text enclosed within a symbol, badge, or seal. Feels official and traditional. Less flexible at small sizes. | Starbucks, Harley-Davidson, BMW, NFL |

> **Design Note:** For most new brands, a **combination mark** is the recommended starting point. It gives the brand both a recognizable symbol and a readable name — and the two elements can be separated for different applications as the brand grows.

---

### 1.3 Qualities of an Effective Logo

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* emphasize that effective graphic design communicates clearly, functions across contexts, and endures over time. Applied to logo design, these standards produce five essential qualities — first articulated by designer Paul Rand and widely adopted in the field:

| Quality | What It Means | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | Uses the fewest elements necessary to communicate the idea | Complex logos fail at small sizes and are hard to remember |
| **Memorable** | Distinct enough to be recalled after a single viewing | Recognition is the primary function of a logo |
| **Timeless** | Avoids trends that will date the design within a few years | A logo should work as well in 20 years as it does today |
| **Versatile** | Works in color, black-and-white, reversed, and at any size | Logos appear on everything from favicons to billboards |
| **Appropriate** | Fits the brand's industry, audience, and personality | A children's toy brand and a law firm require very different visual languages |

> **Critical Thinking:** Look at the Coca-Cola logo. It has remained essentially unchanged since 1887. What qualities make it timeless? What would happen to brand recognition if it were redesigned in a current trend style every five years?

## PART 2: SKETCHING TECHNIQUES (30 minutes)

### 2.1 Why Sketch Before Opening Software?

The instinct of many beginning designers is to open a design application immediately. *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* stress that graphic design is fundamentally a problem-solving discipline — and the best problem-solving happens before any tool is picked up.

Sketching on paper:
- Forces thinking in **concepts**, not in software features
- Allows **20 ideas in the time it takes to build one** on screen
- Makes it cheap and fast to discard bad ideas
- Produces a **record of the design process** — essential for client presentations and revisions
- Removes the temptation to **polish too early**, which locks designers into weak concepts

> **Key Concept:** The goal of sketching is not to produce a finished drawing. It is to externalize as many ideas as possible as quickly as possible, so the best concept can be identified before any time is invested in execution.

---

### 2.2 The Thumbnail Sketching Process

A **thumbnail sketch** is a small, rough drawing — typically 3–5 cm — used to explore a concept quickly. The term comes from the idea that the sketch is roughly the size of a thumbnail.

The structured process below is the professional standard for logo ideation *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*:

**Step 1 — Brief Analysis**
Read the design brief carefully. Highlight key information:
- Industry and business type
- Target audience (age, gender, lifestyle, values)
- Brand personality (e.g., playful, authoritative, minimal, bold)
- Competitors — what do their logos look like? What should this logo do differently?
- Any mandatory elements (colors, symbols, taglines)

**Step 2 — Word Association**
Write 20–30 words related to the brand. Include:
- What the brand *does* (e.g., "coffee," "roast," "morning")
- What the brand *feels like* (e.g., "warm," "ritual," "community")
- What the brand *values* (e.g., "sustainable," "local," "craft")

Circle the 5–8 most visually suggestive words — words that immediately suggest a shape, symbol, or letterform.

**Step 3 — Thumbnail Grid**
Draw a grid of 20 small boxes (4 columns × 5 rows, approximately 3 cm each). Fill every box with a rough mark — one idea per box. Rules:
- No erasing — cross out and move on
- No color at this stage — work in line only
- No judgment — quantity is the goal
- Vary approaches: try a symbol, a letterform, an abstract shape, a combination

**Step 4 — Select and Refine**
Review all 20 thumbnails. Circle the 3–5 strongest concepts. Redraw each selected concept at a larger size (approximately 8–10 cm) with more detail and precision.

**Step 5 — Concept Presentation**
Present 2–3 refined sketches to the client or peer group *before* digitizing. This step prevents wasted digitizing time on a concept the client will reject.

---

### 2.3 Tools for Sketching

| Tool | Best For |
|---|---|
| HB or 2B pencil | Loose thumbnails, quick ideation, easy to see light marks |
| Fine-liner pen (0.3–0.5 mm) | Refined line-art before scanning; clean, consistent line weight |
| Marker or brush pen | Bold, gestural letterforms; testing thick/thin stroke contrast |
| Grid or dot paper | Geometric and symmetrical logos; maintaining proportions |
| Tracing paper | Iterating on top of a base sketch without redrawing from scratch |

---

### 2.4 Sketching Principles from Design Elements

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* identify **line** and **shape** as two of the fundamental elements of graphic design. Both are directly applied in logo sketching:

**Line** — Every logo begins as a line. The character of the line communicates personality:

| Line Type | Communicates |
|---|---|
| Straight horizontal | Stability, calm, groundedness |
| Straight vertical | Strength, authority, formality |
| Diagonal | Energy, movement, dynamism |
| Curved | Friendliness, flow, approachability |
| Jagged / angular | Tension, aggression, excitement |

**Shape** — Enclosed lines form shapes. Shape is the primary carrier of meaning in a logo symbol:

| Shape | Communicates |
|---|---|
| Circle / oval | Unity, wholeness, community, protection, infinity |
| Square / rectangle | Stability, reliability, professionalism, structure |
| Triangle | Direction, power, ambition, tension (point up = strength; point down = instability) |
| Organic / irregular | Nature, creativity, handmade quality, uniqueness |

> **Design Application:** Before sketching, decide what the logo's primary shape language should be. A financial institution should lean toward squares and rectangles (stability). A children's brand should lean toward circles and organic shapes (friendliness). This decision should come from the brief, not from personal preference.

---

### 2.5 Sketching Do's and Don'ts

**✓ DO:**
- Sketch fast — quantity over quality in the thumbnail phase
- Vary your approaches: geometric, organic, typographic, abstract
- Think in silhouette — cover the sketch and check if the shape reads clearly as a solid black form
- Date and number every page of your sketchbook
- Keep all sketches — rejected ideas sometimes become useful later

**✗ DON'T:**
- Don't erase — cross out and move on; erasing wastes time and destroys the ideation record
- Don't add color at the thumbnail stage — color is a distraction from form
- Don't open design software until you have a chosen, refined concept
- Don't show the client only one concept — always present at least two or three directions
- Don't skip the brief analysis — designing without understanding the audience produces generic work

## PART 3: DIGITIZING YOUR SKETCH (30 minutes)

### 3.1 From Paper to Vector

Digitizing is the process of converting a hand-drawn sketch into clean, scalable digital artwork. The goal is not to copy the sketch exactly — it is to **improve it**: refine proportions, smooth curves, correct symmetry, and produce a mark that is technically precise and production-ready.

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* describe graphic design as existing across both print and digital media. A logo must function in both — which means it must be built as a **vector graphic**.

---

### 3.2 Raster vs. Vector — The Fundamental Distinction

Understanding the difference between raster and vector is the most important technical concept in logo production.

| | Raster | Vector |
|---|---|---|
| **Made of** | A fixed grid of pixels (dots of color) | Mathematical paths defined by points and curves |
| **Scaling** | Loses quality when enlarged — becomes pixelated and blurry | Scales to any size with no quality loss — mathematically recalculated |
| **File size** | Larger at high resolution | Typically smaller; resolution-independent |
| **Editing** | Destructive — quality degrades with each edit | Non-destructive — paths can be edited infinitely |
| **File formats** | JPG, PNG, GIF, PSD, TIFF | SVG, AI, EPS, PDF |
| **Logo use** | Web only (PNG with transparency) | All uses — web, print, embroidery, signage, billboard |
| **Software** | Adobe Photoshop, GIMP | Adobe Illustrator, Inkscape, Affinity Designer |

> **Key Rule:** A logo must always be built and stored as a vector. Raster versions (PNG, JPG) are *exports* for specific uses — they are never the master file. A designer who delivers only a JPG logo has not completed the job.

---

### 3.3 The Scan-to-Vector Workflow

**Step 1 — Scan or Photograph the Sketch**
- Scan at **300–600 dpi** in grayscale
- If photographing: use even, diffused lighting with no shadows or perspective distortion
- The sketch should be on clean white paper with dark, confident lines

**Step 2 — Clean Up in a Raster Application**
- Open the scan in Adobe Photoshop (or equivalent)
- Use **Levels** (Ctrl/Cmd + L) to increase contrast: push the black point right and the white point left until lines are crisp and the background is pure white
- Remove smudges, stray marks, and paper texture
- Straighten and crop

**Step 3 — Place as a Template in Illustrator**
- Open Adobe Illustrator and create a new document
- Place the cleaned scan: **File → Place**
- Lock the image on its layer (click the lock icon in the Layers panel)
- Reduce the layer opacity to **40–50%** — this makes the sketch visible but not distracting

**Step 4 — Trace with the Pen Tool**
- Create a new layer above the template layer
- Use the **Pen tool (P)** to draw paths over the sketch
- Build the logo from individual closed shapes — do not use a single continuous outline
- Use as few anchor points as possible — smooth curves need only 2 points

**Step 5 — Refine and Simplify**
- Use the **Direct Selection tool (A)** to adjust individual anchor points and handles
- Use the **Anchor Point tool (Shift + C)** to convert corner points to smooth curves and vice versa
- Align nodes to the pixel grid where appropriate
- Check symmetry: use **Object → Transform → Reflect** to mirror and compare halves
- Delete the template layer when the vector is complete

**Step 6 — Apply Color and Typography**
- Apply brand colors using exact hex, RGB, or CMYK values
- If the logo includes a wordmark, set the type in the chosen typeface
- Outline all text: **Type → Create Outlines** (Shift + Ctrl/Cmd + O) — this converts text to vector paths so the logo is not dependent on installed fonts

---

### 3.4 Key Vector Tools in Adobe Illustrator

| Tool | Shortcut | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Pen tool | P | Draw precise bezier paths — the primary digitizing tool |
| Direct Selection tool | A | Select and move individual anchor points and handles |
| Anchor Point tool | Shift + C | Convert corner points ↔ smooth curve points |
| Pathfinder panel | Window → Pathfinder | Unite, subtract, intersect, and divide overlapping shapes |
| Align panel | Window → Align | Distribute and align objects precisely on a grid |
| Image Trace | Object → Image Trace | Automatically convert a placed bitmap to vector paths |
| Reflect tool | O | Mirror an object across a vertical or horizontal axis |
| Shape Builder tool | Shift + M | Merge or subtract overlapping shapes interactively |

> **Pro Tip — The Pen Tool:** The Pen tool is the most important and most difficult tool in vector design. The key to mastery is using the **fewest anchor points possible**. A smooth curve between two points needs exactly two anchor points — one at each end. Adding extra points creates bumps and makes the path harder to edit. Practice drawing smooth curves with only two points before attempting complex logo shapes.

---

### 3.5 Using Image Trace (Auto-Tracing)

Adobe Illustrator's **Image Trace** function can automatically convert a placed bitmap sketch into vector paths. It is a useful starting point but almost always requires manual cleanup.

**When to use Image Trace:**
- For simple, high-contrast black-and-white sketches
- As a quick first pass to establish the basic path structure
- When the sketch has organic, irregular shapes that are difficult to trace manually

**When NOT to use Image Trace:**
- For logos that require precise geometric construction
- When the sketch has low contrast or complex shading
- As a final result — Image Trace always produces more anchor points than necessary and requires significant simplification

> **Workflow Recommendation:** Use Image Trace to generate a rough vector, then manually simplify and refine the paths. Never deliver an Image Trace result as a finished logo without editing.

## PART 4: SCALABILITY AND FILE FORMATS (20 minutes)

### 4.1 Why Scalability Is Non-Negotiable

A logo appears in an enormous range of sizes and contexts throughout its life:

| Application | Approximate Size |
|---|---|
| Favicon (browser tab) | 16 × 16 px to 32 × 32 px |
| Social media profile picture | 400 × 400 px |
| Website header | 120–300 px wide |
| Business card | 15–25 mm wide |
| Letterhead | 30–50 mm wide |
| Signage / storefront | 300–1,000 mm wide |
| Vehicle wrap | 500–2,000 mm wide |
| Billboard | 3,000–14,000 mm wide |

A raster logo designed at 500 × 500 px will be completely unusable at billboard scale — it will be a blurry, pixelated mess. A vector logo designed at any size will reproduce perfectly at all of the above, because it is not made of pixels — it is made of mathematics.

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* identify **scalability** as a core requirement of professional graphic design output. A design that cannot be reproduced consistently across media has failed a fundamental professional standard.

---

### 4.2 The Scalability Test

Before finalizing any logo, apply the following two-part test:

**Test 1 — Minimum Size**
Print or display the logo at its smallest intended size (typically 15 mm or 57 px wide for a business card). Ask:
- Is the brand name still readable?
- Are all symbol details still visible and distinct?
- Does the overall shape still read clearly?

If the answer to any question is no, the logo is too complex. Simplify until it passes.

**Test 2 — One-Color Silhouette**
Fill the entire logo with a single flat color (black). Remove all gradients, shadows, and color variation. Ask:
- Is the logo still recognizable?
- Does the shape communicate the brand without color support?
- Would it work embroidered on a uniform or engraved on a pen?

If the answer is no, the logo is too dependent on color to carry its meaning. The form must be strong enough to work without color.

> **Key Concept:** Color is an enhancement, not a crutch. The strongest logos — Nike, Apple, McDonald's arches — are instantly recognizable as pure black silhouettes. Build the form first; add color second.

---

### 4.3 Logo Variations to Deliver

A complete professional logo package includes multiple variations to cover every use case:

| Variation | Description | Use Case |
|---|---|---|
| **Full color (primary)** | The logo in its official brand colors | Standard use across all media |
| **Reversed (white)** | White version of the logo for dark backgrounds | Dark website headers, dark packaging, apparel |
| **One-color black** | Solid black version | Fax, photocopying, black-and-white print |
| **One-color white** | Solid white version | Embossing, engraving, white-on-dark print |
| **Horizontal layout** | Logo elements arranged side by side | Website headers, email signatures, banners |
| **Stacked layout** | Logo elements arranged vertically | Square formats, profile pictures, stamps |
| **Icon / symbol only** | The symbol without the wordmark | Favicon, app icon, social media avatar |

---

### 4.4 File Format Guide

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* distinguish between print and digital media as two distinct output environments with different technical requirements. File format selection is the practical application of this distinction.

| Format | Type | Description | Use Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **.AI** | Vector | Adobe Illustrator native file. The master source file — fully editable, all layers intact. | Stored by the designer; used for all future edits |
| **.EPS** | Vector | Encapsulated PostScript. Universal vector format compatible with most professional software. | Print vendors, sign makers, embroidery machines, legacy software |
| **.SVG** | Vector | Scalable Vector Graphics. Open standard vector format readable by web browsers. | Websites, apps, responsive UI, web animation |
| **.PDF** | Vector | Portable Document Format. Preserves vector data when exported correctly from Illustrator. | Print-ready delivery to printers; client presentations |
| **.PNG** | Raster | Portable Network Graphics. Supports transparency (no background). | Web use, presentations, social media, email signatures |
| **.JPG / .JPEG** | Raster | Joint Photographic Experts Group. No transparency; compressed. | Photography, email (when transparency is not needed) |

> **Critical Rule:** The **.AI file is the master**. All other formats are exports derived from it. If the AI file is lost, the logo must be rebuilt from scratch. Always back up the master file in at least two locations.

---

### 4.5 Choosing the Right Format — Quick Reference

| Situation | Correct Format | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sending to a print shop | EPS or PDF | Vector data preserved; universally compatible |
| Placing on a website | SVG or PNG | SVG scales perfectly; PNG provides transparency |
| Sending to an embroidery machine | EPS or AI | Vector paths define stitch lines |
| Creating a favicon | PNG (32×32 px) or ICO | Small raster export from the vector icon |
| Sending to a client for general use | PNG (transparent) + PDF | Readable everywhere; PDF preserves vector quality |
| Storing the master file | AI | Fully editable source |

## PART 5: GROUP ACTIVITY — LOGO CRITIQUE AND REDESIGN (15 minutes)

### Activity Overview

Students are divided into **3 groups**. Each group receives a logo sample and completes a structured analysis and critique.

**Time:** 10 minutes group work + 5 minutes group presentations (approximately 90 seconds each)

---

### Group 1 — The Overly Complex Logo

**Sample:** A logo with multiple colors, a detailed illustration, thin decorative typography, and fine detail that disappears at small sizes.

**Tasks:**
1. Apply the scalability test: describe what happens to this logo at 15 mm wide and in one-color black.
2. Identify which of the five logo qualities (simple, memorable, timeless, versatile, appropriate) it fails and why.
3. Identify the design elements that are causing the problems (line weight, shape complexity, color count, typography choice).
4. Propose a simplified version: what would you remove or change to make it pass the scalability test?
5. Present: *"This logo fails the scalability test because ___. We would simplify it by ___."*

---

### Group 2 — The Trend-Dependent Logo

**Sample:** A logo that uses a popular current style — heavy gradients, 3D effects, or a trendy typeface — saved only as a JPG.

**Tasks:**
1. Identify the design trend being used. Estimate how long this style has been popular and when it might feel dated.
2. Explain why this logo cannot be reproduced in one color or at small sizes.
3. Identify the file format problem: what is missing from a JPG-only delivery?
4. Propose a timeless alternative: what changes would make this logo work in 20 years?
5. Present: *"This logo is trend-dependent because ___. A timeless version would ___."*

---

### Group 3 — The Strong Logo

**Sample:** A logo using a maximum of two colors, a bold geometric symbol, clean typography, and delivered in AI, SVG, EPS, and PNG formats.

**Tasks:**
1. Apply the scalability test: confirm that this logo passes both the minimum size test and the one-color silhouette test.
2. Identify which design elements (line, shape, color, typography) contribute to its strength.
3. Identify the logo type (wordmark, lettermark, brandmark, combination, emblem).
4. Evaluate the file format delivery: is it complete? Is anything missing?
5. Present: *"This logo works because ___. The strongest design decision is ___."*

---

### Presentation Format (90 seconds per group)

Each group shares:
1. The logo they analyzed and its type
2. Whether it passes or fails the scalability test and why
3. One specific strength and one specific weakness
4. Their proposed improvement or confirmation of what makes it effective

## PART 6: SUMMARY AND ASSESSMENT (5 minutes)

### 6.1 Key Takeaways

1. A **logo** is the visual symbol of a brand — not the brand itself. Its power comes from the story and reputation it represents over time *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.
2. The five logo types are: **wordmark, lettermark, brandmark, combination mark, and emblem**. A combination mark is the most versatile starting point for new brands.
3. An effective logo is **simple, memorable, timeless, versatile, and appropriate**. These qualities are not aesthetic preferences — they are functional requirements.
4. **Sketching before software** is the professional standard. The thumbnail grid (20 rough ideas → 5 refined → 2–3 to digitize) produces better concepts faster than designing directly on screen.
5. **Line and shape** are the primary design elements in logo sketching. The character of lines and the geometry of shapes communicate personality before color or typography are applied.
6. Logos must be built as **vector graphics** (AI, EPS, SVG) — not raster images. Vector paths scale to any size without quality loss; raster pixels do not.
7. The **Pen tool** is the primary digitizing tool. Mastery requires using the fewest anchor points possible to produce smooth, clean paths.
8. A complete logo delivery includes: **AI (master), EPS (print), SVG (web), PNG (general use)**, plus all color variations (full color, reversed, one-color black, one-color white).
9. Every logo must pass the **scalability test**: readable at 15 mm wide and recognizable as a one-color black silhouette.

---

### 6.2 Assessment Questions

**Short Answer (Individual — 10 minutes):**

1. Define logo design in your own words. What is the difference between a logo and a brand?
2. A client runs a law firm and asks for a logo that "looks modern and trendy." Using the five qualities of an effective logo, explain why "trendy" is a potential problem and what you would recommend instead.
3. Explain the difference between raster and vector graphics. Why must a logo be built as a vector file?
4. Describe the thumbnail sketching process in your own words. Why is it important to complete 20 thumbnails before refining any single concept?
5. A client receives their logo as a JPG file only. List three specific problems this will cause and identify the correct file formats that should have been delivered instead.

---

### 6.3 Comparison Table: Effective vs. Ineffective Logo Design

| Feature | Effective Logo | Ineffective Logo |
|---|---|---|
| **Complexity** | Simple — minimum elements needed to communicate the idea | Overloaded with detail, gradients, and decorative elements |
| **Scalability** | Reads clearly at 15 mm wide and as a one-color silhouette | Loses detail at small sizes; unrecognizable in one color |
| **File format** | Delivered as AI, EPS, SVG, and PNG with all color variations | Delivered as JPG only — no vector master, no transparency |
| **Timelessness** | Built on strong form and shape — not dependent on current trends | Relies on a trendy style that will date within 3–5 years |
| **Color independence** | The form is strong enough to work without color | Color is carrying the meaning — the form alone is weak |
| **Versatility** | Works on light and dark backgrounds, in color and black-and-white | Only works in one specific color combination |
| **Appropriateness** | Visual language matches the brand's industry and audience | Generic or mismatched — could belong to any brand |
| **Process** | Developed through sketching, concept selection, and refinement | Designed directly in software without ideation or exploration |

### 6.4 Assignment

**Design a Logo from Brief to Delivery**

Complete the full logo design process for one of the following briefs:

- **Brief A:** A local independent coffee shop called *Rooted* — values: community, sustainability, warmth, handcrafted quality. Audience: adults 25–45, urban, environmentally conscious.
- **Brief B:** A children's after-school coding club called *Spark* — values: curiosity, creativity, fun, learning. Audience: children 8–14 and their parents.
- **Brief C:** A freelance photographer's personal brand using their initials — values: precision, artistry, professionalism. Audience: corporate clients and wedding clients.

**Deliverables:**

1. **Word Association List** — 20–30 words related to the brief. Circle the 5–8 most visually suggestive.
2. **Thumbnail Grid** — 20 rough thumbnail sketches (photographed or scanned).
3. **Refined Sketches** — 2–3 selected concepts redrawn at larger scale with more detail.
4. **Final Vector Logo** — The chosen concept digitized in Adobe Illustrator (or equivalent vector software). Must include:
   - Full color version
   - One-color black version
   - One-color white version (shown on a dark background)
5. **Written Reflection (5–7 sentences):** Identify the logo type, explain the shape and line choices, describe the color psychology, and confirm that the logo passes the scalability test.

**Submission:** Next class meeting

**Evaluation Criteria:**

| Criterion | Description |
|---|---|
| Process documentation | Word association list and thumbnail grid demonstrate genuine ideation |
| Concept strength | The chosen concept is simple, appropriate, and passes the scalability test |
| Vector quality | Paths are clean, anchor points are minimal, shapes are precise |
| Color variations | All three color versions are delivered correctly |
| Written reflection | Accurately identifies logo type, design decisions, and scalability confirmation |
