## PART 1: WHAT IS A SOCIAL MEDIA CAMPAIGN? (25 minutes)

### 1.1 Definition

A **social media campaign** is a coordinated series of graphic and written content published across one or more social media platforms — such as Facebook, Instagram, X (Twitter), TikTok, LinkedIn, or YouTube — with a specific, measurable goal in mind.

According to *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)*, digital graphic design is no longer limited to print — it has expanded into every screen a person interacts with daily. Social media is now one of the most powerful channels for visual communication, and the designer's role is to create graphics that stop the scroll, communicate instantly, and drive action.

A campaign is different from a single post. A **single post** is one piece of content. A **campaign** is a planned, unified series of posts that:
- Share a consistent visual identity (colors, fonts, style)
- Communicate a single core message across multiple touchpoints
- Are designed to build recognition and trust over time
- Have a defined start date, end date, and goal

**Examples of campaign goals:**
- Product launch — introduce a new item to the market
- Brand awareness — make more people recognize and remember the brand
- Event promotion — drive attendance or registration
- Sales promotion — push a discount, offer, or limited-time deal
- Social cause — raise awareness or funds for an advocacy

---

### 1.2 Why Design Matters in Social Media

The average person scrolls through **300 feet of content per day** on social media — roughly the height of the Statue of Liberty. In that environment, a graphic has approximately **1.7 seconds** to capture attention before the viewer scrolls past.

This means social media design is not decorative — it is **functional communication under extreme time pressure**.

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* emphasize that effective graphic design in digital media must achieve three things simultaneously:

| Goal | What It Means for Design |
|---|---|
| **Stop the scroll** | The visual must be striking enough to interrupt automatic scrolling behavior |
| **Communicate instantly** | The core message must be understood within 2 seconds, even without reading the caption |
| **Drive action** | The design must guide the viewer toward a specific next step — click, share, save, buy |

> **Key Insight:** A beautiful graphic that does not communicate clearly is a failed design. In social media, clarity always beats complexity.

---

### 1.3 The Anatomy of a Social Media Graphic

Every effective social media graphic — whether a promotional post, story, or carousel — is built from the same core components:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  BRAND MARK / LOGO          (top corner) │
│                                          │
│         HERO IMAGE / VISUAL              │
│      (dominant visual element)           │
│                                          │
│         HEADLINE / KEY MESSAGE           │
│         (largest, most prominent text)   │
│                                          │
│         Supporting text / subtext        │
│         (smaller, secondary info)        │
│                                          │
│  [ CALL TO ACTION BUTTON / TEXT ]        │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Component | Function |
|---|---|
| **Brand Mark / Logo** | Establishes ownership and builds recognition — always present |
| **Hero Visual** | The dominant image, illustration, or graphic that draws the eye first |
| **Headline** | The single most important message — must be readable at a glance |
| **Supporting Text** | Secondary information — price, date, details, tagline |
| **Call to Action (CTA)** | Tells the viewer what to do next — "Shop Now," "Learn More," "Register Today" |

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Overloading a social media graphic with too much text. Research consistently shows that posts with minimal text and a strong visual outperform text-heavy posts. The caption handles the details — the graphic handles the hook.

---

### 1.4 Types of Social Media Content Formats

Social media platforms support multiple content formats, each with different design requirements:

| Format | Description | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| **Static Post** | A single image — the most common format | Announcements, quotes, product shots |
| **Carousel** | Multiple swipeable images in one post | Step-by-step guides, product ranges, storytelling |
| **Story / Reel Cover** | Vertical full-screen format (9:16 ratio) | Time-sensitive content, behind-the-scenes, promotions |
| **Infographic** | Data or information presented visually | Educational content, statistics, how-tos |
| **Animated GIF / Motion Graphic** | Looping or short animated content | Attention-grabbing promotions, product demos |
| **Cover Photo / Banner** | Large header image for profile pages | Brand identity, event promotion, seasonal updates |

---

### 1.5 Platform Specifications (2025 Standards)

Every platform has specific image size requirements. Designing at the wrong size results in cropped, blurry, or distorted graphics.

| Platform | Post Size | Story Size | Profile Image |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Instagram** | 1080 × 1080 px (1:1) or 1080 × 1350 px (4:5) | 1080 × 1920 px (9:16) | 320 × 320 px |
| **Facebook** | 1200 × 630 px (link) or 1080 × 1080 px (square) | 1080 × 1920 px | 170 × 170 px |
| **X (Twitter)** | 1200 × 675 px (16:9) | — | 400 × 400 px |
| **LinkedIn** | 1200 × 627 px (post) or 1128 × 191 px (banner) | — | 400 × 400 px |
| **TikTok** | 1080 × 1920 px (9:16 video) | 1080 × 1920 px | 200 × 200 px |
| **YouTube** | 2560 × 1440 px (channel art) | — | 800 × 800 px |

> **Design Tip:** Always design at the largest required size and scale down. Never scale a small graphic up — it will pixelate. Design at **72–96 PPI** for screen output.

## PART 2: DESIGN PRINCIPLES FOR SOCIAL MEDIA CAMPAIGNS

Effective social media design is not random — it is built on the same foundational principles of graphic design, applied specifically to the constraints and behaviors of digital platforms *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.

---

### 2.1 Visual Consistency — The Campaign Identity System

A campaign is only as strong as its visual consistency. When every post in a campaign looks like it belongs to the same family, the brand builds **recognition** — viewers begin to identify the brand before they even read the name.

**A campaign identity system includes:**

| Element | What It Controls |
|---|---|
| **Color Palette** | 2–4 colors used consistently across all posts |
| **Typography** | 1–2 font families used for all headlines and body text |
| **Logo Placement** | Same position (usually top-left or top-right) on every post |
| **Image Style** | Consistent photography style — same filter, lighting, or illustration style |
| **Layout Grid** | Consistent margins, padding, and element placement |
| **Tone of Voice** | The visual "mood" — playful, serious, luxurious, minimal |

**Real-World Example:** Coca-Cola's social media campaigns always use the same red, the same Spencerian script logo, and the same warm, joyful photography style — regardless of the platform or the specific campaign message. You recognize it before you read a single word.

> 💬 **Class Discussion:** Open Instagram and find a brand you follow. Can you identify their campaign identity system? What colors, fonts, and image styles do they use consistently?

---

### 2.2 Visual Hierarchy in Social Media Design

As established in the previous module, **visual hierarchy** controls what the viewer sees first, second, and last. In social media design, hierarchy is even more critical because the viewer is moving fast and has no obligation to stop.

**The Social Media Hierarchy Rule:**

```
01 — HERO VISUAL          ← Stops the scroll
02 — HEADLINE             ← Communicates the message
03 — SUPPORTING TEXT      ← Provides context or detail
04 — CALL TO ACTION       ← Directs the next step
05 — BRAND MARK           ← Confirms the source
```

Every element must earn its place. If an element does not serve one of these five roles, it should be removed.

**Applying hierarchy to social media:**
- The **hero visual** should occupy the majority of the frame — at least 50–60% of the space
- The **headline** should be the largest text element — readable even as a thumbnail
- **Supporting text** should be noticeably smaller — it is for viewers who are already engaged
- The **CTA** should be visually distinct — a button shape, a contrasting color, or bold weight
- The **logo** should be present but not dominant — it should not compete with the headline

---

### 2.3 Color in Social Media Design

Color is the first thing the eye processes — before shape, before text. In social media, color is the primary tool for stopping the scroll *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.

**Color strategies for social media:**

| Strategy | How It Works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Brand Color Dominance** | Use the brand's primary color as the dominant background or accent | Spotify's black + green on every post |
| **Contrast for CTA** | Make the call-to-action button a color that contrasts sharply with the background | Orange CTA on a blue background |
| **Seasonal Palette Shifts** | Adjust the palette for seasons or campaigns while keeping brand colors present | Christmas red added to a normally blue palette |
| **Color for Emotion** | Choose colors that match the emotional tone of the message | Warm orange for an energetic sale; cool blue for a trust-building post |
| **Monochromatic Campaigns** | Use one color in multiple shades for a sophisticated, unified look | A luxury brand using 5 shades of navy |

> ⚠️ **Platform Consideration:** Screens display color in **RGB mode**. Always design social media graphics in RGB, never CMYK. CMYK is for print only — colors will look dull and inaccurate on screen if designed in CMYK.

---

### 2.4 Typography in Social Media Design

Typography on social media must work under three constraints that do not apply to print:
1. **Small screen sizes** — text must be legible on a 5-inch phone screen
2. **Fast viewing** — the headline must be readable in under 2 seconds
3. **Compression artifacts** — JPEG compression can blur thin, delicate fonts

**Typography rules for social media:**

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| **Minimum 24px for headlines** | Anything smaller becomes unreadable on mobile |
| **Maximum 2 font families per post** | More than 2 creates visual noise and inconsistency |
| **High contrast between text and background** | Low contrast text disappears on small screens |
| **Avoid thin/light font weights for small text** | Thin strokes compress poorly and become illegible |
| **Left-align body text** | Centered body text is harder to read at speed |
| **Use ALL CAPS sparingly** | Effective for short labels; exhausting for full sentences |

**Typographic hierarchy example for a promotional post:**

```
SALE ENDS SUNDAY        ← 64px, Bold, White — Headline
Up to 50% off           ← 36px, SemiBold, Yellow — Subheading
Selected items only     ← 18px, Regular, Light Grey — Fine print
[ SHOP NOW ]            ← 20px, Bold, Button — CTA
```

---

### 2.5 Layout and Composition

The layout of a social media graphic determines how the eye moves through the content. *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* identify composition as one of the most critical skills in digital design — a poorly composed graphic loses the viewer even if the content is strong.

**Key composition principles for social media:**

**The Rule of Thirds**
Divide the canvas into a 3×3 grid. Place the most important elements along the grid lines or at their intersections — not dead center. This creates natural visual tension and interest.

```
┌───┬───┬───┐
│   │ ✦ │   │   ✦ = Strong placement points
├───┼───┼───┤       (intersections of grid lines)
│   │   │   │
├───┼───┼───┤
│   │   │ ✦ │
└───┴───┴───┘
```

**Safe Zones**
Keep all critical text and logos within the **inner 80%** of the canvas. Platform interfaces (profile icons, action buttons, captions) can overlap the edges of your graphic, cutting off content placed too close to the border.

**Breathing Room (White Space)**
Every element needs space around it. Crowded social media graphics feel cheap and are harder to read. White space is not wasted space — it is what makes the important elements feel important.

**Visual Flow**
Design the graphic so the eye moves naturally from the hero visual → headline → supporting text → CTA. Use size, color, and placement to create this path deliberately.

---

### 2.6 The 3-Second Rule

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* note that digital audiences make snap judgments about content. In social media design, the **3-second rule** states:

> If a viewer cannot understand the core message of your graphic within 3 seconds, the design has failed — regardless of how beautiful it is.

**Testing your design against the 3-second rule:**
1. Show the design to someone for exactly 3 seconds, then hide it.
2. Ask: "What was this graphic about? What was it selling or saying?"
3. If they cannot answer correctly, the hierarchy, headline, or visual is not clear enough.

**Common reasons a design fails the 3-second test:**
- Too much text competing for attention
- Headline is too small or low contrast
- The hero visual does not relate clearly to the message
- Too many colors or elements creating visual noise
- The CTA is missing or buried

## PART 3: BANNER ADS — DESIGN FOR DIGITAL ADVERTISING (25 minutes)

### 3.1 What is a Banner Ad?

A **banner ad** (also called a *display ad*) is a rectangular graphic advertisement embedded within a website, app, or digital platform. It is one of the oldest and most widely used forms of digital advertising, and despite the rise of social media, banner ads remain a dominant format in digital marketing.

According to *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)*, banner ads are a direct application of graphic design principles to commercial communication — every pixel must work toward a single goal: getting the viewer to click.

Banner ads appear in three primary contexts:
- **Website display advertising** — embedded in news sites, blogs, and content platforms
- **Search engine advertising** — image ads shown alongside Google search results
- **In-app advertising** — displayed within mobile applications

---

### 3.2 Standard Banner Ad Sizes

The **Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB)** defines standard banner ad sizes used across the web. Designers must know these sizes because advertisers and platforms require specific dimensions.

| Ad Name | Dimensions | Common Placement |
|---|---|---|
| **Leaderboard** | 728 × 90 px | Top of webpage, above content |
| **Medium Rectangle** | 300 × 250 px | Sidebar, within article content |
| **Large Rectangle** | 336 × 280 px | Sidebar, within article content |
| **Half Page** | 300 × 600 px | Right sidebar — high visibility |
| **Wide Skyscraper** | 160 × 600 px | Right or left sidebar |
| **Billboard** | 970 × 250 px | Top of high-traffic pages |
| **Mobile Banner** | 320 × 50 px | Top or bottom of mobile screens |
| **Large Mobile Banner** | 320 × 100 px | Top or bottom of mobile screens |

> **Design Reality:** The **300×250 Medium Rectangle** is the single most used banner ad size on the internet. If you only learn one banner size, learn this one. It performs well across desktop and mobile and is accepted by virtually every ad network.

---

### 3.3 The Anatomy of a Banner Ad

A banner ad is a compressed version of a full advertisement. It must communicate a complete message in a very small space — sometimes as narrow as 728×90 pixels.

Every banner ad contains the same core components:

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  LOGO    │    HEADLINE / KEY MESSAGE    │  [ CTA BUTTON ] │
│          │    Supporting text           │                  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
           (Example: 728 × 90 Leaderboard)
```

For taller formats (300×250, 300×600):

```
┌─────────────────────┐
│   LOGO              │
│                     │
│   HERO IMAGE        │
│                     │
│   HEADLINE          │
│   Supporting text   │
│                     │
│  [ CALL TO ACTION ] │
└─────────────────────┘
```

| Component | Function | Design Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Logo** | Brand identification | Always present — top corner or bottom |
| **Headline** | Core message in 5–8 words maximum | Largest text element |
| **Supporting Text** | Offer detail, tagline, or product name | Smaller — only if space allows |
| **Hero Image / Visual** | Product, person, or graphic that supports the message | Must be immediately recognizable |
| **CTA Button** | The clickable action — "Buy Now," "Learn More," "Get Offer" | High contrast, clearly a button |
| **Background** | Sets the tone and frames all other elements | Must not compete with the content |

---

### 3.4 Design Principles Specific to Banner Ads

Banner ads have unique design constraints that go beyond standard graphic design. *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* note that digital advertising design requires an understanding of both visual communication and user behavior.

**Constraint 1 — Extreme Size Limitations**

A leaderboard banner (728×90 px) is only 90 pixels tall. That is roughly 2.4 cm on a standard monitor. Every element must be ruthlessly prioritized. There is no room for decoration — only communication.

**Rules for small banner formats:**
- Maximum 1 headline, 1 supporting line, 1 CTA, 1 logo
- No body paragraphs — ever
- Font size minimum: 14px for supporting text, 20px+ for headline
- CTA button must be at least 30px tall to be tappable on mobile

**Constraint 2 — Banner Blindness**

**Banner blindness** is a well-documented phenomenon where internet users unconsciously ignore banner ads because they have learned to filter them out. Research shows that the average click-through rate (CTR) for display ads is **0.1%** — meaning 999 out of 1,000 people who see a banner do not click it.

Designers combat banner blindness through:

| Technique | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Unexpected visuals** | An image that breaks the pattern of typical ads — unusual, funny, or striking |
| **Motion / Animation** | Animated banners attract 3× more attention than static ones |
| **High contrast** | A banner that stands out sharply from the surrounding webpage content |
| **Minimal text** | A banner with one bold headline is more readable than one with five lines of text |
| **Personalization cues** | Content that feels relevant to the viewer (retargeting ads) |

**Constraint 3 — File Size Limits**

Ad networks impose strict file size limits on banner ads to prevent slow page loading:
- Static image (JPG/PNG): maximum **150 KB**
- Animated GIF: maximum **150 KB**
- HTML5 animated ad: maximum **200 KB** initial load

This means designers must optimize images aggressively — compressing without visible quality loss.

**Constraint 4 — The Click Zone**

The entire banner is typically clickable, but the **CTA button** must make this obvious. A viewer should never have to wonder whether a banner is clickable. Design cues that signal clickability:
- A clearly shaped button with rounded corners
- A contrasting color that stands out from the rest of the banner
- Action-oriented text: "Shop Now," "Get 20% Off," "Download Free"
- A subtle drop shadow or border on the button

---

### 3.5 Static vs. Animated Banner Ads

Banner ads come in two primary forms: static and animated.

**Static Banner Ads**
- A single, non-moving image
- Simpler to design and produce
- Lower file size — easier to meet ad network limits
- Best for: simple offers, brand awareness, clean product shots

**Animated Banner Ads**
- Multiple frames that cycle in a loop (GIF or HTML5)
- Attract significantly more attention than static ads
- Can tell a short sequential story (Problem → Solution → CTA)
- Best for: product demonstrations, multi-message campaigns, sales with countdown timers

**Animation best practices:**
- Loop maximum **3 times** — looping indefinitely becomes annoying
- Each frame should be readable on its own — viewers may only see one frame
- The **final frame** must always show the CTA and logo — this is the frame most viewers see
- Animation speed: minimum **1 second per frame** — faster is unreadable
- Avoid flashing or strobing effects — these are banned by most ad networks and cause accessibility issues

**Animated Banner Frame Structure:**

```
Frame 1 (2 sec) → Problem / Hook        "Tired of slow internet?"
Frame 2 (2 sec) → Solution / Offer      "Switch to FiberMax — 1Gbps"
Frame 3 (3 sec) → CTA + Logo            "Get Connected Today  [Sign Up]"
                                          ← This frame stays longest
```

---

### 3.6 The Relationship Between Banner Ads and Landing Pages

A banner ad does not exist in isolation — it is the **first step in a conversion journey**. The banner's job is to get the click. The **landing page** it links to must then close the deal.

*Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)* emphasize that visual consistency between the ad and the landing page is critical. When a viewer clicks a banner and arrives at a page that looks completely different, they experience **visual disconnect** — a jarring inconsistency that destroys trust and increases bounce rates.

**Message Match Principle:**
The headline on the banner ad must match or closely echo the headline on the landing page. The color palette, imagery, and offer must be consistent.

| Banner Ad Says | Landing Page Must Show |
|---|---|
| "50% Off All Shoes This Weekend" | A page showing shoes at 50% off — not the homepage |
| "Download Your Free Guide" | A page with the download form — not a blog post |
| "Book a Free Consultation" | A booking form — not a general services page |

> ⚠️ **Design Failure:** A beautifully designed banner ad that links to a mismatched or generic landing page wastes the entire advertising budget. The designer must consider the full journey, not just the ad.

## PART 4: THE CAMPAIGN DESIGN PROCESS (15 minutes)

Designing a social media campaign or banner ad series is not just about making individual graphics look good. It requires a structured process that begins with strategy and ends with evaluation *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.

---

### 4.1 Step 1 — Define the Campaign Brief

Before opening any design software, the designer must understand the campaign's purpose. A **campaign brief** answers:

| Question | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| **What is the goal?** | Awareness, sales, event sign-ups, followers — each goal requires a different design approach |
| **Who is the audience?** | Age, gender, lifestyle, and values determine visual style, tone, and platform choice |
| **What is the key message?** | One sentence that the entire campaign communicates — everything else is secondary |
| **What platforms will it run on?** | Determines sizes, formats, and technical requirements |
| **What is the timeline?** | Campaign start/end dates, posting schedule |
| **What are the brand guidelines?** | Colors, fonts, logo rules, tone of voice |

> **Rule:** A designer who skips the brief produces beautiful work that solves the wrong problem.

---

### 4.2 Step 2 — Establish the Visual Identity for the Campaign

Even if the brand already has a visual identity, each campaign needs its own **campaign-specific visual layer** — a set of design choices that make this campaign feel distinct while remaining consistent with the brand.

**Campaign visual identity decisions:**
- **Hero color** — one dominant color that defines this campaign (e.g., a summer sale campaign uses bright coral)
- **Campaign typeface** — a display font used only for this campaign's headlines
- **Campaign imagery style** — the specific type of photos or illustrations used
- **Graphic motif** — a recurring shape, pattern, or icon used across all posts
- **Campaign tagline** — a short phrase that appears on every piece of content

---

### 4.3 Step 3 — Design the Content Hierarchy

Before designing individual posts, map out the **content hierarchy** of the entire campaign:

```
CAMPAIGN HERO POST          ← The most important post — launches the campaign
    ↓
SUPPORTING POSTS (3–6)      ← Reinforce the message from different angles
    ↓
REMINDER / URGENCY POST     ← "Last chance," countdown, deadline reminder
    ↓
CLOSING POST                ← Thank you, results, or transition to next campaign
```

Each post type has a different design priority:
- The **hero post** must be the most visually striking — it sets the tone for everything that follows
- **Supporting posts** can be simpler — they build on the established visual language
- The **urgency post** must use high-contrast, action-oriented design — red, bold text, countdown elements
- The **closing post** can be warmer and more personal — it builds relationship, not just sales

---

### 4.4 Step 4 — Design, Test, and Optimize

**Design phase:**
- Create all assets at the correct platform sizes
- Ensure every post passes the 3-second rule
- Check that all text is legible at mobile size (view at 25% zoom to simulate a phone screen)
- Verify the logo is present and correctly placed on every asset

**Testing phase:**
- View all posts together — do they look like a unified campaign?
- Check color consistency across different screen types (phone, tablet, desktop)
- Test animated assets for loop count and frame timing
- Verify file sizes meet platform and ad network requirements

**Optimization phase:**
After the campaign runs, analyze performance data:
- Which post had the highest engagement? Why?
- Which banner ad had the highest click-through rate? What design elements contributed?
- Which post was saved or shared most? What made it valuable?

> **Design Insight:** Data from a live campaign is the most honest feedback a designer can receive. A post that performs poorly despite looking beautiful is telling you something important about your audience.

## PART 5: GROUP ACTIVITY — CAMPAIGN CRITIQUE & DESIGN SPRINT (20 minutes)

### Activity Overview

Students are divided into **4 groups**. Each group is assigned a different task — two groups critique existing campaigns, two groups complete a design sprint.

**Time:** 13 minutes group work + 7 minutes presentations (1–2 min per group)

---

### Group 1 — Social Media Campaign Critique

**Task:** Analyze a real brand's social media campaign (instructor provides 3–5 screenshots from a brand's Instagram or Facebook feed).

**Questions to answer:**
1. What is the campaign's goal? How do you know from the design alone?
2. Identify the campaign's visual identity system — colors, fonts, imagery style, logo placement.
3. Does every post pass the 3-second rule? Which one is strongest? Which is weakest?
4. Is there a clear call to action on each post? Is it visually prominent?
5. What would you change about one post to make it more effective?

**Present:** *"This campaign works because ___ and could be improved by ___."*

---

### Group 2 — Banner Ad Critique

**Task:** Analyze 3 banner ads provided by the instructor (printed or projected — different sizes and brands).

**Questions to answer:**
1. Identify the ad format/size for each banner. What placement would it appear in?
2. For each banner: identify the logo, headline, supporting text, hero visual, and CTA.
3. Which banner is most effective? Rank them 1–3 and justify your ranking using design principles.
4. Does any banner suffer from banner blindness risk? What would you change?
5. Are the CTAs clear and compelling? Suggest an improvement for the weakest one.

**Present:** *"The most effective banner is ___ because ___ and the weakest is ___ because ___."*

---

### Group 3 — Campaign Design Sprint (Social Media)

**Task:** Design a mini social media campaign concept for a fictional brand (instructor assigns one from the Logo Design Activity client briefs).

**Deliverables (sketched or described — no software required):**
1. Define the campaign goal in one sentence.
2. Choose a campaign color palette (3 colors) and explain why.
3. Sketch or describe the layout of the **hero post** — what is the hero visual, headline, and CTA?
4. Describe 2 supporting posts — what angle does each one take?
5. Write the headline for each post (maximum 8 words each).

**Present:** *"Our campaign for [brand] communicates ___ through ___ and targets ___ audience."*

---

### Group 4 — Banner Ad Design Sprint

**Task:** Design a banner ad concept for the same fictional brand assigned to Group 3.

**Deliverables (sketched or described — no software required):**
1. Choose one banner size and explain why (from the IAB standard sizes).
2. Sketch the layout — where is the logo, headline, visual, and CTA?
3. Write the headline (maximum 6 words) and CTA text (maximum 3 words).
4. Choose a background color and CTA button color — explain the contrast strategy.
5. Decide: static or animated? If animated, describe the 3 frames.

**Present:** *"Our banner ad for [brand] uses ___ format because ___ and the CTA is ___ because ___."*

## PART 6: SUMMARY & ASSESSMENT (5 minutes)

### 6.1 Key Takeaways

1. A **social media campaign** is a coordinated series of visually consistent graphics designed to achieve a specific goal across one or more platforms *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.
2. Social media design must **stop the scroll, communicate instantly, and drive action** — all within approximately 1.7 seconds of viewer attention.
3. Every social media graphic has five components: **hero visual, headline, supporting text, CTA, and brand mark**.
4. **Visual consistency** across all campaign posts builds brand recognition — colors, fonts, logo placement, and image style must be unified.
5. A **banner ad** is a rectangular digital advertisement designed to appear within websites and apps, with the primary goal of generating clicks.
6. The most important banner ad size is the **300×250 Medium Rectangle** — it is the most widely used format on the internet.
7. **Banner blindness** is a real phenomenon — designers combat it with unexpected visuals, animation, high contrast, and minimal text.
8. Animated banners should loop a **maximum of 3 times**, and the **final frame** must always show the CTA and logo.
9. The **3-second rule** applies to both social media posts and banner ads — if the message is not clear in 3 seconds, the design has failed.
10. **Message match** between a banner ad and its landing page is critical — visual disconnect destroys trust and increases bounce rates.

### 6.2 Comparison Table: Social Media Post vs. Banner Ad

| Feature | Social Media Post | Banner Ad |
|---|---|---|
| **Platform** | Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, LinkedIn, etc. | Websites, apps, search engines |
| **Primary Goal** | Engagement, awareness, community building | Clicks, conversions, direct response |
| **Viewer Behavior** | Scrolling through a feed — can stop and engage | Browsing a webpage — ad is peripheral |
| **Text Amount** | Minimal on graphic; caption handles detail | Extremely minimal — 5–8 words maximum |
| **File Format** | JPG, PNG, MP4, GIF | JPG, PNG, GIF, HTML5 |
| **File Size Limit** | Platform-dependent (usually 30MB for video) | Strict — 150KB for static, 200KB for HTML5 |
| **Animation** | Optional but effective | Highly recommended — 3× more attention |
| **CTA** | Text or button within the graphic | Always a clearly designed button |
| **Size Standards** | Platform-specific (1080×1080, 1080×1920, etc.) | IAB standard sizes (300×250, 728×90, etc.) |
| **Design Lifespan** | Hours to days — content moves fast | Days to weeks — ads run on a schedule |
