## PART 1: WHAT IS TYPOGRAPHY? (25 minutes)

### 1.1 Definition

**Typography** is the art and technique of arranging type to make written language **legible, readable, and visually appealing**. It is one of the most powerful tools in a designer's toolkit — it communicates not just *what* is said, but *how* it feels.

According to *Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)*, typography in graphic design serves three simultaneous functions:

| Function | What It Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 🗣️ **Communication** | Delivers the written message clearly and efficiently | A headline that is readable at a glance |
| 🎭 **Expression** | Conveys tone, personality, and emotion through letterform | A horror film title in jagged, distressed type |
| 🏗️ **Structure** | Organizes information into a visual hierarchy | A newspaper with headline, deck, byline, and body |

> **Key Concept:** Typography is never neutral. Every typeface choice sends a message before a single word is read. A luxury brand using Comic Sans and a children's toy brand using Times New Roman both feel immediately wrong — because the typeface contradicts the message.

---

### 1.2 Typography vs. Font vs. Typeface

These three terms are frequently confused. Understanding the distinction is essential for professional communication.

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Typeface** | The overall design of a set of letterforms — the family | *Helvetica*, *Garamond*, *Futura* |
| **Font** | A specific instance of a typeface at a particular weight and style | *Helvetica Bold*, *Helvetica Light Italic* |
| **Typography** | The practice of arranging type in a design — the craft | Choosing, sizing, spacing, and placing type on a layout |

> 💬 **Analogy:** A typeface is like a song. A font is like a specific recording of that song (acoustic version, live version, remix). Typography is the act of curating a playlist.

---

### 1.3 The Five Typeface Classifications

Every typeface belongs to a classification that reflects its historical origin, structural characteristics, and appropriate use context *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.

#### Classification 1: Serif

Serifs are the small finishing strokes at the ends of letterform strokes. Serif typefaces feel **traditional, authoritative, and trustworthy**.

```
T  ← serifs visible at the top and bottom of the stroke
```

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Defining Feature** | Small horizontal strokes (serifs) at the ends of letterforms |
| **Mood / Personality** | Classic, formal, authoritative, trustworthy, literary |
| **Best Used For** | Books, newspapers, academic documents, law firms, luxury brands |
| **Common Examples** | Times New Roman, Garamond, Georgia, Palatino, Baskerville |
| **Avoid For** | Very small sizes on screen, casual or playful brands |

**Sub-classifications:**
- **Old Style** (Garamond) — low contrast, angled stress, humanist feel
- **Transitional** (Baskerville) — moderate contrast, more refined
- **Modern / Didone** (Bodoni) — extreme thick/thin contrast, high fashion
- **Slab Serif** (Rockwell, Courier) — thick, block-like serifs, industrial feel

---

#### Classification 2: Sans-Serif

"Sans" means "without" in French. Sans-serif typefaces have **no finishing strokes** — clean, geometric, modern.

```
T  ← no serifs, clean terminal strokes
```

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Defining Feature** | No serifs — clean, unadorned stroke endings |
| **Mood / Personality** | Modern, clean, minimal, neutral, approachable, tech-forward |
| **Best Used For** | Digital interfaces, tech brands, signage, body text on screen |
| **Common Examples** | Helvetica, Arial, Futura, Gill Sans, Roboto, Open Sans |
| **Avoid For** | Long-form print body text (can feel cold and tiring over many pages) |

**Sub-classifications:**
- **Grotesque** (Helvetica, Arial) — early sans-serifs, slightly irregular
- **Geometric** (Futura, Avenir) — based on perfect circles and lines
- **Humanist** (Gill Sans, Myriad) — influenced by calligraphy, warmer feel
- **Neo-Grotesque** (Roboto, Inter) — refined grotesque, optimized for screens

---

#### Classification 3: Script

Script typefaces simulate **handwriting or calligraphy**. They feel personal, elegant, or casual depending on the style.

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Defining Feature** | Letterforms that connect or appear hand-drawn |
| **Mood / Personality** | Elegant, personal, romantic, creative, artisanal |
| **Best Used For** | Wedding invitations, luxury packaging, logos, greeting cards |
| **Common Examples** | Pacifico, Great Vibes, Lobster, Brush Script, Edwardian Script |
| **Avoid For** | Body text, long passages, small sizes, all-caps settings |

> ⚠️ **Rule:** Never set body text in a script typeface. Script is for display use only — headlines, logos, short accent phrases. At small sizes or in long passages, script becomes completely illegible.

---

#### Classification 4: Display / Decorative

Display typefaces are designed for **large sizes and short text** — they prioritize visual impact over readability at small sizes.

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Defining Feature** | Highly stylized, unique, often themed or conceptual |
| **Mood / Personality** | Varies widely — bold, quirky, retro, futuristic, horror, playful |
| **Best Used For** | Posters, headlines, logos, titles, event branding |
| **Common Examples** | Impact, Bebas Neue, Playfair Display, Abril Fatface |
| **Avoid For** | Body text, captions, anything below 24px |

---

#### Classification 5: Monospace

Every character in a monospace typeface occupies **exactly the same horizontal width**.

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Defining Feature** | Fixed-width characters — every letter takes the same space |
| **Mood / Personality** | Technical, code-like, retro, mechanical, precise |
| **Best Used For** | Code samples, terminal interfaces, typewriter aesthetics, tabular data |
| **Common Examples** | Courier New, Consolas, JetBrains Mono, Space Mono |
| **Avoid For** | General body text, headlines, anything requiring elegant spacing |

---

### 1.4 Typeface Classification Summary Table

| Classification | Serifs? | Mood | Best Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Serif** | ✔ Yes | Traditional, authoritative | Print body text, formal documents | Very small screen text |
| **Sans-Serif** | ✗ No | Modern, clean, neutral | Screen UI, signage, tech | Long-form print body |
| **Script** | Varies | Elegant, personal, romantic | Display, logos, invitations | Body text, small sizes |
| **Display** | Varies | Bold, expressive, themed | Headlines, posters, titles | Body text, small sizes |
| **Monospace** | Varies | Technical, mechanical | Code, tabular data | General body text |

> 💬 **Class Discussion:** Look at five brand logos around you (on your phone, clothing, or classroom). What typeface classification does each use? Does the classification match the brand's personality?

## PART 2: FONT PAIRING PRINCIPLES (35 minutes)

### 2.1 What is Font Pairing?

**Font pairing** is the practice of selecting two or more typefaces that work together harmoniously in a single design. A well-paired combination creates visual interest through contrast while maintaining cohesion through shared qualities *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.

> **The Golden Rule of Font Pairing:** Fonts should be different enough to create contrast, but similar enough to feel like they belong together.

Most professional designs use **two typefaces** — one for display/headlines and one for body text. Rarely three. Almost never four or more.

| Number of Fonts | Guideline |
|---|---|
| **1 font** | Use different weights and styles for hierarchy — clean and disciplined |
| **2 fonts** | The professional standard — one display, one body |
| **3 fonts** | Acceptable with strong justification — one display, one body, one accent |
| **4+ fonts** | Almost always a mistake — creates visual chaos |

---

### 2.2 The Five Font Pairing Strategies

#### Strategy 1: Serif + Sans-Serif (The Classic Pair)

The most reliable and widely used pairing in professional design. The contrast between the structured serif and the clean sans-serif creates natural hierarchy and visual interest.

```
HEADLINE IN SERIF          ← authoritative, traditional
Body text in sans-serif.   ← clean, readable, modern
```

| Role | Typeface Type | Why It Works |
|---|---|---|
| Headline / Display | Serif | Commands attention, feels established |
| Body / Supporting | Sans-Serif | Easy to read, modern, doesn't compete |

**Classic Examples:**
- Playfair Display (Serif) + Source Sans Pro (Sans-Serif)
- Merriweather (Serif) + Open Sans (Sans-Serif)
- Georgia (Serif) + Arial (Sans-Serif)

> **Why It Works:** The serif headline carries authority and visual weight. The sans-serif body text is clean and readable. The contrast between them creates hierarchy without conflict.

---

#### Strategy 2: Sans-Serif + Sans-Serif (The Modern Pair)

Two sans-serif typefaces can work together when they come from **different sub-classifications** or have significantly different weights.

```
GEOMETRIC SANS HEADLINE    ← structured, bold, impactful
Humanist sans body text.   ← warm, readable, approachable
```

**Rules for Sans + Sans Pairing:**
- Use typefaces from **different sub-classifications** (geometric + humanist)
- Create contrast through **weight** (Black headline + Regular body)
- Avoid pairing two typefaces that look nearly identical (e.g., Arial + Helvetica)

**Examples:**
- Futura Bold (Geometric) + Gill Sans Regular (Humanist)
- Montserrat Black (Geometric) + Lato Regular (Humanist)
- Bebas Neue (Display Sans) + Roboto Regular (Neo-Grotesque)

---

#### Strategy 3: Serif + Serif (The Editorial Pair)

Two serif typefaces can work together when they come from **different historical periods** or have contrasting proportions.

```
MODERN SERIF HEADLINE      ← high contrast, elegant, fashion
Old style serif body text. ← warm, literary, comfortable to read
```

**Rules for Serif + Serif Pairing:**
- Pair a **high-contrast modern serif** (Bodoni, Didot) with a **low-contrast old style** (Garamond, Caslon)
- Avoid pairing two serifs from the same sub-classification
- Use significant size difference to reinforce the distinction

**Examples:**
- Bodoni (Modern) + Garamond (Old Style)
- Playfair Display (Transitional) + Lora (Old Style)

---

#### Strategy 4: Display + Body (The Expressive Pair)

A bold, expressive display typeface paired with a neutral, highly readable body typeface. The display font carries the personality; the body font carries the content.

```
EXPRESSIVE DISPLAY TITLE   ← personality, brand voice, impact
Neutral body text here.    ← invisible, serves the content
```

**Rules:**
- The body typeface must be **completely neutral** — it should not compete with the display font
- The display font should only appear at **large sizes** (headlines, titles, pull quotes)
- Never use the display font for body text

**Examples:**
- Abril Fatface + Lato
- Lobster + Open Sans
- Bebas Neue + Source Sans Pro

---

#### Strategy 5: One Typeface, Multiple Weights (The Disciplined Pair)

Using a single typeface family with multiple weights and styles to create hierarchy. This is the most cohesive approach — everything comes from the same design DNA.

```
Typeface Black 900          ← dominant headline
Typeface SemiBold 600       ← subheading
Typeface Regular 400        ← body text
Typeface Light 300 Italic   ← caption / accent
```

**Best Typeface Families for This Approach:**
- **Roboto** — Thin, Light, Regular, Medium, Bold, Black (+ Italic variants)
- **Montserrat** — 9 weights available
- **Source Sans Pro** — 6 weights
- **Helvetica Neue** — extensive weight range

> **When to Use:** Brand systems, UI design, minimalist editorial layouts where visual consistency is more important than expressive contrast.

---

### 2.3 Font Pairing: What to Avoid

| ❌ Mistake | Why It Fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Two similar fonts** | Creates confusion, not contrast (Arial + Helvetica) | Choose fonts from different classifications |
| **Two expressive fonts** | Both compete for attention — visual chaos | One expressive, one neutral |
| **Two script fonts** | Illegible and overwhelming | Maximum one script per design |
| **Mismatched moods** | A playful font + a formal font feel schizophrenic | Match the emotional register of both fonts |
| **Too many fonts** | 4+ fonts = amateur, chaotic | Maximum 2–3 fonts per design |
| **Random pairing** | No shared quality to unify them | Find a shared trait: x-height, era, proportion |

---

### 2.4 The Shared Quality Rule

Every successful font pair shares **at least one quality** that creates a subtle visual connection, even while contrasting in other ways.

| Shared Quality | Example |
|---|---|
| **Similar x-height** | Both fonts have tall lowercase letters — they feel proportionally related |
| **Same historical era** | Both fonts designed in the same period share structural DNA |
| **Similar proportions** | Both fonts are condensed, or both are wide — they feel like a family |
| **Complementary moods** | One is formal, one is approachable — they balance each other |
| **Designed together** | Some type designers create paired families (e.g., Alegreya + Alegreya Sans) |

> 💬 **Class Discussion:** Open Google Fonts. Find two typefaces you think would pair well. Explain what shared quality connects them and what contrasting quality creates interest between them.

---

### 2.5 Font Pairing in Context: Industry Examples

| Industry | Headline Font | Body Font | Why It Works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Luxury Fashion** | Didot (Modern Serif) | Futura Light (Geometric Sans) | Extreme contrast — high fashion elegance meets geometric precision |
| **Tech Startup** | Montserrat Bold (Geometric Sans) | Inter Regular (Neo-Grotesque) | Clean, modern, screen-optimized, professional |
| **Editorial / Magazine** | Playfair Display (Transitional Serif) | Source Sans Pro (Humanist Sans) | Classic editorial authority with clean readability |
| **Children's Brand** | Fredoka One (Rounded Display) | Nunito Regular (Rounded Sans) | Shared rounded quality — playful and cohesive |
| **Law / Finance** | Garamond (Old Style Serif) | Times New Roman (Transitional Serif) | Both formal, authoritative, traditional — same register |
| **Food / Artisan** | Playfair Display Italic | Lato Regular | Elegant headline with warm, approachable body |

## PART 3: TYPOGRAPHY LAYOUT PRINCIPLES (25 minutes)

### 3.1 What is a Typographic Layout?

A **typographic layout** is the deliberate arrangement of type on a page or screen to create a clear, readable, and visually organized composition. Layout decisions determine not just where text sits, but how the viewer's eye moves through it *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.

A strong typographic layout controls six variables:

| Variable | What It Controls |
|---|---|
| **Hierarchy** | Which text is most important and seen first |
| **Alignment** | How text is positioned relative to a grid or edge |
| **Spacing** | Distance between letters, words, lines, and blocks |
| **Grid** | The invisible structure that organizes all elements |
| **Measure** | The width of a text column |
| **Contrast** | Visual difference between typographic levels |

---

### 3.2 Alignment

Alignment is one of the most fundamental layout decisions in typography. It determines the visual edge of text and affects the overall tone of the design.

#### The Four Alignment Types

**1. Left-Aligned (Ragged Right)**

```
This is left-aligned text.
Each line starts at the same
left edge, but ends at
different points on the right.
```

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Natural, readable, informal to neutral |
| **Best For** | Body text, articles, most general-purpose layouts |
| **Why It Works** | Matches the natural left-to-right reading direction; consistent left edge guides the eye |
| **Avoid** | Very long lines without a defined column width |

---

**2. Right-Aligned (Ragged Left)**

```
          This is right-aligned text.
    Each line ends at the same right
              edge, but starts at
         different points on the left.
```

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Sophisticated, unconventional, directional |
| **Best For** | Captions next to left-side images, pull quotes, accent text |
| **Why It Works** | Creates a strong right edge that anchors text to a visual element |
| **Avoid** | Body text — the ragged left edge makes it hard to find the start of each line |

---

**3. Centered**

```
        This is centered text.
    Each line is centered on the
          same vertical axis.
```

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Formal, ceremonial, balanced, classic |
| **Best For** | Invitations, certificates, headlines, short display text |
| **Why It Works** | Creates symmetry and a sense of occasion |
| **Avoid** | Body text longer than 3–4 lines — the ragged edges on both sides make it exhausting to read |

---

**4. Justified**

```
This  is  justified  text.  Every
line  fills  the  full  column  width
by  adjusting  the  spacing  between
words  to  create  clean  edges.
```

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Feel** | Formal, structured, editorial, newspaper-like |
| **Best For** | Multi-column print layouts, books, newspapers, magazines |
| **Why It Works** | Creates clean left and right edges — looks organized and professional |
| **Avoid** | Narrow columns (creates "rivers" of white space); digital body text without hyphenation |

> ⚠️ **Justified Text Warning:** Justified text in narrow columns creates uneven word spacing — sometimes called "rivers" — where gaps align vertically through a paragraph, creating a distracting visual path. Always use hyphenation with justified text in print.

---

### 3.3 Spacing: The Three Levels

Typography has three distinct levels of spacing, each affecting readability and visual rhythm differently.

#### Level 1: Tracking (Letter-Spacing)

**Tracking** is the uniform spacing between all characters in a word or block of text.

| Tracking Value | Effect | Best Used For |
|---|---|---|
| **Tight / Negative** | Letters closer together — dense, compact | Display headlines, bold impact text |
| **Normal / 0** | Default spacing — balanced and readable | Body text, most general use |
| **Loose / Positive** | Letters further apart — airy, elegant | ALL CAPS labels, subheadings, luxury brands |

> **Rule:** Never apply loose tracking to lowercase body text — it destroys readability. Loose tracking works best on ALL CAPS or short display text.

```
TIGHT TRACKING HEADLINE     ← powerful, compact
N O R M A L  T R A C K I N G  ← elegant, spacious (ALL CAPS only)
Normal tracking body text.  ← readable, balanced
```

---

#### Level 2: Kerning (Pair-Specific Spacing)

**Kerning** is the adjustment of space between **specific pairs of characters** to correct optical imbalances. Unlike tracking, kerning is applied to individual letter pairs, not entire blocks.

Common problem pairs:
```
AV  →  A V  (too much space optically)
To  →  T o  (the T overhangs the o)
WA  →  W A  (both letters angle inward)
```

| Kerning Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Optical Kerning** | Software adjusts spacing based on the visual shape of each letter pair |
| **Metric Kerning** | Uses the kerning values built into the font by its designer |
| **Manual Kerning** | Designer adjusts specific pairs by hand — used for logos and display type |

> **Rule:** Always check kerning on headlines and logos. Body text at small sizes does not require manual kerning — the eye compensates automatically.

---

#### Level 3: Leading (Line Spacing)

**Leading** (pronounced "ledding") is the vertical space between lines of text — measured from baseline to baseline.

| Leading Value | Effect | Best Used For |
|---|---|---|
| **Tight (< 1.2×)** | Lines feel cramped — hard to read | Display headlines only |
| **Normal (1.2–1.5×)** | Comfortable for headlines and subheadings | Headings, short text blocks |
| **Comfortable (1.5–1.8×)** | Optimal for body text readability | Body text, articles, books |
| **Loose (> 2×)** | Airy, spacious — feels premium | Pull quotes, luxury layouts |

**Formula:** Body text leading = font size × 1.5 to 1.6

```
Example: 16px body text → 24px–26px leading (line height)
```

> **Rule:** Leading that is too tight makes text feel suffocating and hard to track from line to line. Leading that is too loose makes the text feel disconnected and hard to follow as a paragraph.

---

### 3.4 Measure (Column Width)

**Measure** is the width of a text column. It is one of the most overlooked but most important factors in readability.

The optimal measure for body text is **45–75 characters per line** (including spaces). This is based on how far the eye can comfortably travel before needing to return to the left edge.

| Measure | Characters Per Line | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Too Narrow** | < 40 characters | Eye returns too frequently — choppy, tiring |
| **Optimal** | 45–75 characters | Comfortable reading rhythm |
| **Too Wide** | > 85 characters | Eye loses its place returning to the next line |

> **Practical Rule:** A column of body text should be approximately **the width of the alphabet written out twice** (52 characters) as a starting point.

---

### 3.5 The Typographic Grid

A **typographic grid** is an invisible framework of horizontal and vertical lines that organizes all type and visual elements on a page. It creates consistency, alignment, and visual order *(Mrantinkovic et al., 2020)*.

#### Grid Components

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Columns** | Vertical divisions that define text column widths |
| **Gutters** | Spaces between columns — prevent text from running together |
| **Margins** | Space between the content area and the page edge |
| **Baseline Grid** | Horizontal lines spaced at the leading interval — all text sits on these lines |
| **Modules** | Rectangular units formed by the intersection of columns and rows |

#### Common Grid Systems

| Grid Type | Structure | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| **Single Column** | One wide text column | Books, long-form articles, essays |
| **Two Column** | Two equal or unequal columns | Newsletters, brochures, academic papers |
| **Three Column** | Three equal columns | Magazines, newspapers, websites |
| **Modular Grid** | Columns + rows creating a matrix of modules | Complex layouts, editorial design, UI |
| **Manuscript Grid** | Single large text block with wide margins | Books, reports, formal documents |

> 💬 **Class Discussion:** Open a magazine or newspaper. Can you identify the grid? How many columns does it use? Where are the gutters? Does every element align to the grid?

---

### 3.6 Typographic Hierarchy in Layouts

A complete typographic layout typically uses **four to five distinct levels** of hierarchy. Each level must be visually distinct from the others.

#### The Five-Level Typographic System

```
LEVEL 1 — HEADLINE
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Largest · Boldest · Highest contrast · Most dominant
Seen first · Communicates the core message

LEVEL 2 — Subheading
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Smaller than headline · Still bold or semibold
Introduces sections · Supports the headline

LEVEL 3 — Body Text
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Regular weight · Comfortable size (16–18px)
Carries the main content · Optimized for reading

LEVEL 4 — Caption / Label
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Smaller than body · Often italic or light weight
Describes images · Provides supplementary detail

LEVEL 5 — Fine Print / Footnote
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Smallest · Lightest · Least contrast
Legal text · References · Disclaimers
```

**Example System (Print):**

| Level | Size | Weight | Color | Tracking |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Headline | 64px | Black 900 | #000000 | -20 |
| Subheading | 28px | SemiBold 600 | #1a1a1a | 0 |
| Body | 16px | Regular 400 | #333333 | 0 |
| Caption | 13px | Regular 400 | #666666 | +20 |
| Fine Print | 10px | Light 300 | #999999 | +10 |

---

### 3.7 Common Typographic Layout Patterns

#### Pattern 1: The Editorial Layout

Used in magazines, newspapers, and long-form articles. Combines a dominant headline with a multi-column body text grid.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                         │
│   LARGE DOMINANT HEADLINE               │
│   Subheading that supports the story    │
│                                         │
│  ┌──────────────┐  ┌──────────────┐    │
│  │ Body text    │  │ Body text    │    │
│  │ column one   │  │ column two   │    │
│  │ continues    │  │ continues    │    │
│  └──────────────┘  └──────────────┘    │
│  Caption text below image               │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Key Principles:** Strong headline hierarchy, multi-column body, consistent baseline grid, captions below images.

---

#### Pattern 2: The Poster Layout

A single dominant typographic element supported by minimal secondary text. Maximum visual impact.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                         │
│                                         │
│         MASSIVE HEADLINE                │
│         FILLS THE SPACE                 │
│                                         │
│              Subtext here               │
│                                         │
│         DATE · VENUE · TIME             │
│                                         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Key Principles:** One dominant element, extreme size contrast, generous white space, minimal text levels.

---

#### Pattern 3: The Web / UI Layout

Optimized for screen reading — left-aligned, generous leading, clear visual hierarchy, responsive column widths.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  LOGO                    NAV LINKS      │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                         │
│  Hero Headline Here                     │
│  Supporting subheading text             │
│  [ Call to Action Button ]              │
│                                         │
├──────────┬──────────┬───────────────────┤
│ Card 1   │ Card 2   │ Card 3            │
│ Title    │ Title    │ Title             │
│ Body     │ Body     │ Body              │
└──────────┴──────────┴───────────────────┘
```

**Key Principles:** Left-aligned body text, 16–18px minimum body size, 1.5–1.6 line height, clear CTA hierarchy.

---

#### Pattern 4: The Centered Formal Layout

Used for invitations, certificates, diplomas, and formal announcements. Symmetrical and ceremonial.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                         │
│         INSTITUTION NAME                │
│                                         │
│      This is to certify that            │
│                                         │
│         RECIPIENT NAME                  │
│                                         │
│   has successfully completed...         │
│                                         │
│         DATE          SIGNATURE         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Key Principles:** All text centered, serif typefaces, generous spacing between levels, formal hierarchy.

## PART 4: GROUP ACTIVITY — FONT PAIRING & LAYOUT CRITIQUE (20 minutes)

### Activity Overview

Students are divided into **4 groups**. Each group receives a design brief and completes a structured pairing and critique task.

**Time:** 13 minutes group work + 7 minutes presentations (1–2 min per group)

---

### Group 1 — Font Pairing Challenge

**Brief:** A new artisan coffee brand called *Roast & Ritual* needs a typographic identity. The brand is warm, handcrafted, and premium — targeting adults aged 25–40 who value quality over convenience.

**Tasks:**
1. Choose a font pair (headline + body) from the five pairing strategies. Name the specific typefaces.
2. Identify which pairing strategy you used and why it fits the brand.
3. Describe the mood each font communicates and how they complement each other.
4. Identify one font pair that would be **wrong** for this brand and explain why.
5. Present: *"We chose ___ + ___ because ___ and it fits the brand because ___."*

**Guide Questions:**
- Does the headline font feel warm and artisanal, or cold and corporate?
- Does the body font feel readable and approachable?
- Do the two fonts share a quality that connects them?

---

### Group 2 — Layout Alignment Analysis

**Brief:** You are given three versions of the same text block — one left-aligned, one centered, one justified.

**Tasks:**
1. Identify which alignment works best for each of these contexts: a wedding invitation, a news article, a product description on a website.
2. Explain why each alignment choice fits or fails its context.
3. Find one example of mismatched alignment in a real design (on your phone or in a magazine) and explain the problem.
4. Present: *"For a wedding invitation, ___ alignment works because ___. For a news article, ___ works because ___."*

**Guide Questions:**
- Does the alignment match the tone of the content?
- Does the alignment create a clean, readable edge?
- Would a different alignment improve or harm the design?

---

### Group 3 — Spacing Audit

**Brief:** You are given a printed paragraph with poor spacing — tight leading, no tracking variation, and inconsistent margins.

**Tasks:**
1. Identify the three spacing problems (tracking, leading, measure).
2. Specify the corrected values: what leading, tracking, and column width would you apply?
3. Explain how each correction improves readability.
4. Sketch or describe the corrected paragraph layout.
5. Present: *"The spacing problems were ___ and we fixed them by ___."*

**Guide Questions:**
- Is the leading tight enough to feel cramped, or loose enough to feel disconnected?
- Is the column too wide (eye loses its place) or too narrow (too many line breaks)?
- Does the tracking feel appropriate for the font size and weight?

---

### Group 4 — Typographic Layout Redesign

**Brief:** A local community center has a flyer with all text in one size, one weight, one color, centered, with no grid structure.

**Tasks:**
1. Identify the five typographic levels the flyer needs (headline, subheading, body, caption, CTA).
2. Assign a font, size, weight, and color to each level.
3. Choose a layout pattern (editorial, poster, centered formal) and explain why.
4. Sketch the redesigned layout showing the grid, columns, and hierarchy.
5. Present: *"We redesigned the layout using ___ pattern because ___ and the hierarchy is now ___."*

**Guide Questions:**
- What is the single most important piece of information on the flyer?
- How many typographic levels does the content actually need?
- Which layout pattern best serves the purpose of a community flyer?

---

### Presentation Format (1–2 min per group)

Each group shares:
1. The brief they worked on
2. Their key decision and the reasoning behind it
3. One thing they would do differently with more time

## PART 5: SUMMARY & ASSESSMENT (15 minutes)

### 5.1 Key Takeaways

1. **Typography** is the art of arranging type — it communicates not just content but tone, personality, and hierarchy.
2. The five typeface classifications are **Serif, Sans-Serif, Script, Display, and Monospace** — each with distinct moods and appropriate use cases.
3. **Font pairing** works through contrast + shared quality. The five strategies are: Serif + Sans-Serif, Sans + Sans, Serif + Serif, Display + Body, and One Family Multiple Weights.
4. The **Golden Rule:** Maximum 2–3 fonts per design. One expressive, one neutral.
5. **Alignment** choices (left, right, centered, justified) affect both readability and tone — match alignment to context.
6. **Spacing** operates at three levels: tracking (letter-spacing), kerning (pair-specific), and leading (line spacing).
7. **Measure** — the optimal column width is 45–75 characters per line for body text.
8. A **typographic grid** provides the invisible structure that makes layouts feel organized and professional.
9. A complete typographic layout uses **4–5 distinct hierarchy levels** — each must be visually different from the others.

---

### 5.2 Assessment Questions

**Short Answer (Individual — 10 minutes):**

1. What is the difference between a typeface, a font, and typography? Give one example of each.
2. A designer pairs Lobster (Script) with Pacifico (Script) for a restaurant menu. What is wrong with this pairing? What would you recommend instead and why?
3. Explain the difference between **tracking** and **kerning**. When would you use each?
4. A body text column has 110 characters per line. What problem does this create and how would you fix it?
5. A poster uses centered alignment for a 200-word body text block. What readability problem does this create? Which alignment would you use instead?
6. Name the five pairing strategies and give one real-world example of each being used effectively.

---

### 5.3 Comparison Table: Effective vs. Ineffective Typography

| Feature | Effective Typography | Ineffective Typography |
|---|---|---|
| **Font Count** | 2–3 fonts maximum | 4+ fonts competing for attention |
| **Pairing Logic** | Contrast + shared quality | Random or near-identical fonts |
| **Hierarchy** | 4–5 visually distinct levels | All text looks the same size and weight |
| **Alignment** | Matches the content's tone and context | Centered body text, justified narrow columns |
| **Leading** | 1.5–1.6× font size for body text | Too tight (cramped) or too loose (disconnected) |
| **Measure** | 45–75 characters per line | Too wide (eye loses place) or too narrow (choppy) |
| **Tracking** | Loose only on ALL CAPS display text | Loose tracking on lowercase body text |
| **Grid** | All elements align to a consistent grid | Elements placed arbitrarily |
| **Viewer Experience** | Reads naturally, hierarchy is clear | Confusing, tiring, unprofessional |

### 5.4 Quick Reference: Font Pairing Cheat Sheet

| Pairing Strategy | Headline | Body | Mood |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serif + Sans-Serif** | Playfair Display | Source Sans Pro | Editorial, authoritative |
| **Serif + Sans-Serif** | Merriweather | Open Sans | Warm, readable, versatile |
| **Sans + Sans** | Montserrat Black | Lato Regular | Modern, clean, tech |
| **Sans + Sans** | Futura Bold | Gill Sans Regular | Geometric, humanist balance |
| **Display + Body** | Abril Fatface | Lato | Expressive, bold, neutral body |
| **One Family** | Roboto Black | Roboto Regular | Cohesive, disciplined, UI |
| **Serif + Serif** | Bodoni | Garamond | High fashion, editorial luxury |

### 5.5 Assignment

**Create a Typographic Layout System**

Using any design tool (Canva, Adobe Express, Figma, Photoshop, or hand-drawn):

**Deliverable 1 — Font Pair Specimen (40 points)**

Design a single-page **type specimen** that showcases your chosen font pair. Include:
- Font pair name and classification of each font
- Pairing strategy used (name it)
- All five hierarchy levels demonstrated: Headline, Subheading, Body, Caption, Fine Print
- A short paragraph of body text showing the body font at its optimal size and leading
- A label explaining why the pair works (2–3 sentences)

**Deliverable 2 — Typographic Layout (60 points)**

Design a **one-page layout** for one of the following:
- A poster for a school event
- A one-page magazine article spread
- A product landing page (web layout)
- A formal certificate or invitation

Requirements:
- Use your font pair from Deliverable 1
- Apply a visible grid (show column lines in your source file)
- Include at least **4 typographic hierarchy levels**
- Apply correct alignment for the layout type
- Body text must be 45–75 characters per line
- Leading must be 1.5–1.6× the body font size

**Deliverable 3 — Written Reflection (included in layout)**

A short text block within the layout (or on a separate page) answering:
- What pairing strategy did you use and why?
- What alignment did you choose and why does it fit the layout type?
- What grid structure did you use?

**Submission:** Next class meeting
**Format:** PNG or PDF export + source file (.PSD, .fig, .canva, or scanned hand-drawn)

**Rubric:**

| Criterion | Points |
|---|---|
| Font pair is clearly from one of the five strategies | 10 |
| All five hierarchy levels are visually distinct | 15 |
| Alignment is appropriate for the layout type | 10 |
| Leading and measure are within optimal ranges | 10 |
| Grid is visible and all elements align to it | 15 |
| Layout is visually cohesive and professional | 15 |
| Written reflection accurately describes decisions | 10 |
| Submission includes both export and source file | 5 |
| **TOTAL** | **100** |
