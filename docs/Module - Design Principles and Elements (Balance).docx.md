## PART 1: OVERVIEW OF DESIGN ELEMENTS AND PRINCIPLES (30 minutes)

### 1.1 What Are Design Elements?

Design elements are the **basic building blocks** used to create any visual composition. According to the *3G Handy Guide: Graphic Designing* (Mrantinkovic et al., 2020), these elements form the foundation of all graphic design work.

| Element | Description | Real-World Example | Critical Thinking |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Line** | A mark connecting two points; creates direction, movement, and structure | The curved ribbon lines in the Coca-Cola logo create flow and elegance | What feeling does a diagonal line give vs. a horizontal line? |
| **Shape** | A two-dimensional enclosed area defined by lines or color | The smile/arrow shape in the Amazon logo connects 'a' to 'z' | Why do circles feel friendlier than sharp triangles? |
| **Color** | Hue, saturation, and value used to evoke emotion and create contrast | Coca-Cola's high-saturation red creates urgency and energy | How would Coca-Cola feel if its red were replaced with pale pink? |
| **Texture** | The visual or tactile surface quality of a design | A rough stone texture in a poster vs. a smooth gradient background | Can texture exist in a digital design? How? |
| **Space** | The area around, between, or within elements (positive and negative space) | The generous white space around the Apple logo signals premium quality | What happens to a design that has no negative space at all? |
| **Form** | Three-dimensional representation of shape (height, width, depth) | A product package box or a 3D rendered logo with shadows and highlights | How does adding depth (form) change how a viewer perceives a flat shape? |
| **Value** | The lightness or darkness of a color or tone | Samsung's deep navy blue has a low value, conveying authority and trust | How does value create the illusion of depth in a flat design? |
| **Typography** | The style, arrangement, and appearance of text | Amazon's bold lowercase font feels approachable; a serif font feels formal | If you changed a logo's font, would it still feel like the same brand? |

Ask students to identify design elements in a sample poster or logo shown on screen.


#### Understanding Color: Hue, Saturation, and Value

- **Hue** — The pure color itself: red, blue, yellow, green, etc. It is what we commonly refer to as "color" in everyday language. Hues can be primary (red, blue, yellow), secondary (orange, green, violet), or tertiary (a mix of primary and secondary).
- **Saturation** — The intensity or vividness of a color. A highly saturated color appears bold and vivid; a low-saturation color appears muted, grey, or washed out. Saturation controls how dominant a color feels in a composition.
- **Value** — The lightness or darkness of a color. Adding white to a hue creates a **tint** (raises the value); adding black creates a **shade** (lowers the value). Value creates depth, contrast, and visual hierarchy in a design.

### 1.2 What Are Design Principles?

Design principles are the **guidelines** that govern how elements are arranged and used together to create effective visual communication (Mrantinkovic et al., 2020).

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **Balance** | The equal distribution of visual weight in a composition. Creates stability and harmony. Three types: *symmetrical* (mirrored), *asymmetrical* (different but equal weight), and *radial* (radiating from a center point). |
| **Contrast** | The use of opposing elements — dark vs. light, large vs. small, rough vs. smooth — to create visual interest and hierarchy. Without contrast, nothing stands out. It is the primary tool for guiding where the viewer looks first. |
| **Emphasis** | Deliberately making one element the focal point of a design through size, color, placement, or contrast. Answers the question: *"Where should the viewer look first?"* |
| **Movement** | The path the viewer's eye travels through a design. Lines, shapes, color gradients, and directional cues (arrows, a person's gaze) create implied movement, guiding the viewer through content in a deliberate order. |
| **Repetition** | Repeating visual elements — colors, fonts, shapes, patterns — to create consistency and rhythm. Builds brand identity and makes a design feel unified rather than random. |
| **Proportion** | The size relationship between elements relative to each other and to the whole composition. Correct proportion creates harmony; intentional mis-proportion creates drama or emphasis. The *Golden Ratio* (1:1.618) is a classic proportion used widely in design. |
| **Unity** | The sense that all elements in a design belong together — forming one cohesive whole rather than a collection of unrelated parts. Achieved through consistent use of color, style, spacing, and repetition. |
| **White Space** | The empty area around, between, and within design elements. Not wasted space — it gives elements room to breathe, improves readability, and directs focus. Designs with generous white space feel premium and modern. |

> **Note:** All 8 principles work together. Balance provides stability, contrast creates interest, emphasis sets priority, movement guides flow, repetition builds consistency, proportion maintains harmony, unity ties everything together, and white space gives it all room to breathe. Balance is considered one of the most fundamental principles because it affects the overall stability and visual comfort of a design.

## PART 2: BALANCE IN GRAPHIC DESIGN (60 minutes)

### 2.1 What Is Balance?

**Balance** is the principle of design that refers to the **equal distribution of visual weight** within a composition. It creates a sense of stability, harmony, and order (Mrantinkovic et al., 2020).

Visual weight is determined by:
- **Size** – Larger elements feel heavier
- **Color** – Dark or saturated colors feel heavier than light ones
- **Texture** – Highly textured areas attract more visual attention
- **Position** – Elements placed farther from the center carry more visual weight
- **Quantity** – Multiple small elements can balance one large element

> **Key Concept:** Balance does NOT mean everything must be equal in size or placement — it means the overall composition feels visually stable.

---

### 2.2 Types of Balance

#### A. SYMMETRICAL BALANCE (Formal Balance)

Symmetrical balance occurs when elements on **both sides of a central axis are mirror images** of each other.

- Creates a sense of **formality, stability, and elegance**
- Commonly used in: logos, formal invitations, government seals, religious art
- Can feel **static or rigid** if overused

**Example:** A butterfly's wings, a human face, the Taj Mahal facade.

```
[ LEFT SIDE ]  |  [ RIGHT SIDE ]
   (mirror image of each other)
```

> **Activity:** Show 3 examples of symmetrical designs. Ask students to identify the axis of symmetry.

---

#### B. ASYMMETRICAL BALANCE (Informal Balance)

Asymmetrical balance occurs when **different elements on each side of the axis have equal visual weight** but are not mirror images.

- Creates a sense of **dynamism, energy, and modernity**
- More complex and visually interesting than symmetrical balance
- Requires careful arrangement of size, color, and space
- Commonly used in: magazine layouts, web design, modern advertising

**How to achieve asymmetrical balance:**
- A large element on one side balanced by several small elements on the other
- A dark-colored element balanced by a larger light-colored element
- A textured area balanced by a large area of white space

**Example:** A large image on the left balanced by a block of text and smaller images on the right.

> **Activity:** Students sketch a simple asymmetrical layout using basic shapes (circle, square, triangle) on paper. Share and discuss.

---

#### C. RADIAL BALANCE

Radial balance occurs when elements **radiate outward from a central point**, like spokes on a wheel.

- Creates a sense of **movement, energy, and focus**
- The center becomes the natural focal point
- Commonly used in: mandalas, sunburst designs, circular logos, clock faces

**Example:** A sunflower, a dartboard, a compass rose.

```
         ↑
      ↖  |  ↗
    ←  CENTER  →
      ↙  |  ↘
         ↓
```

> **Discussion:** Where do you commonly see radial balance in everyday life? (Wheels, flowers, pizza slices, etc.)

---

### 2.3 Comparison Table: Types of Balance

| Feature | Symmetrical | Asymmetrical | Radial |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mirror Image?** | Yes | No | No |
| **Axis** | Vertical/Horizontal | Any | Central point |
| **Mood/Feel** | Formal, stable | Dynamic, modern | Energetic, focused |
| **Complexity** | Simple | Complex | Moderate |
| **Common Use** | Logos, formal design | Web, editorial | Circular designs |

---

### 2.4 Balance and Visual Weight

Understanding **visual weight** is essential to achieving balance. The following factors affect how heavy an element appears:

1. **Size** – A large shape appears heavier than a small one
2. **Color** – Warm, dark, or saturated colors feel heavier
3. **Isolation** – An element surrounded by space draws more attention
4. **Complexity** – Detailed or textured elements feel heavier
5. **Position** – Elements at the top or edges of a composition feel heavier

> **Mrantinkovic et al. (2020)** emphasize that a designer must develop an intuitive sense of visual weight to create compositions that feel naturally balanced without being rigid.

## PART 3: APPLICATION AND ANALYSIS (20 minutes)

### 3.1 Analyzing Balance in Real Designs

**Activity: Design Critique (Group Work)**

Divide the class into 3 groups. Each group receives a printed or projected design sample:
- Group 1: Analyze a **symmetrical** design (e.g., a corporate logo)
- Group 2: Analyze an **asymmetrical** design (e.g., a magazine cover)
- Group 3: Analyze a **radial** design (e.g., a mandala or circular poster)

Each group answers:
1. What type of balance is used?
2. What elements contribute to the visual weight?
3. Does the design feel balanced? Why or why not?
4. How would you improve the balance?

---

### 3.2 Common Mistakes in Balance

- Placing all heavy elements on one side without compensation
- Using too many competing focal points
- Ignoring negative/white space as a balancing element
- Relying only on symmetry when asymmetry would be more effective
- Forgetting that color and texture also carry visual weight

## PART 4: SUMMARY AND ASSESSMENT (10 minutes)

### 4.1 Key Takeaways

1. **Design elements** (line, shape, color, texture, space, form, value, typography) are the building blocks of visual design.
2. **Design principles** guide how elements are arranged to create effective compositions.
3. **Balance** is the distribution of visual weight to create stability and harmony.
4. The three types of balance are:
   - **Symmetrical** – mirror image, formal, stable
   - **Asymmetrical** – different but equal weight, dynamic, modern
   - **Radial** – radiating from center, energetic, focused
5. Visual weight is influenced by size, color, texture, position, and complexity.

---

### 4.2 Assessment Questions

**Short Answer (Individual):**

1. Define balance in graphic design in your own words.
2. What is the difference between symmetrical and asymmetrical balance?
3. Give one real-world example of radial balance.
4. List three factors that affect visual weight in a composition.
5. Why is balance considered a fundamental principle of design?

---

### 4.3 Assignment (Take-Home)

**Create a Design Composition:**

Using any drawing or design tool (Canva, Adobe Express, MS Paint, or hand-drawn):
- Create **three versions** of the same composition (a simple poster or layout)
  - Version 1: Symmetrical balance
  - Version 2: Asymmetrical balance
  - Version 3: Radial balance
- Label each version and write 2–3 sentences explaining how balance was achieved.

**Submission:** Next class meeting
